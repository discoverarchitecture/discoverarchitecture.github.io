Brutalist architecture

262 buildings

Brutalist architecture uses exposed, unpainted concrete or brick, as an ethic of honest materials as much as a look. Swedish architect Hans Asplund coined the word nybrutalism for a brick house in Uppsala designed in 1950. Alison Smithson first printed "new brutalism" in 1953, and the critic Reyner Banham used it in a 1955 essay to name both an ethic and a style. The name is often traced to béton brut, Le Corbusier's phrase for his rough concrete. The style spread around the world, and its popularity began to fall in the late 1970s.

Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation in Marseille (1952) is often cited as the style's first inspiration: a vertical neighbourhood of duplex flats served by internal streets. Park Hill in Sheffield (1961) takes those streets outside as wide concrete decks that follow the slope. Boston City Hall (1968) is an inverted ziggurat whose form shows the city government's work from outside. The Kenyatta International Convention Centre in Nairobi (1973) sets a cylindrical tower beside a conical-roofed hall. The Barbican Centre in London (1982) buries an arts centre below a podium of walkways, lakes and bush-hammered towers.

Questions students ask

What is Brutalist architecture?

Brutalism is a style of concrete or brick left bare and unpainted, and its idea of truthful materials is a principle as well as an appearance. Many link the name to béton brut, the term Le Corbusier used for rough concrete. The Swedish architect Hans Asplund invented the term nybrutalism to describe a brick house designed in 1950; Alison Smithson put "new brutalism" into print in 1953, and Reyner Banham, a critic, took it up in a 1955 essay.

What are the key features of Brutalism?

Its main mark is concrete or brick left exposed and unpainted. Many brutalist buildings also put their structure and circulation on show. In Sheffield, Park Hill runs broad deck "streets in the sky" along its blocks; Balfron Tower in London places its lifts in a separate tower, joined to the flats by bridges on every third floor; and Boston City Hall's inverted ziggurat makes the work of city government visible on its exterior.

Which brutalist buildings should architecture students study?

Begin with Marseille's Unité d'Habitation (1952) by Le Corbusier, which many cite as the first spark for the style: duplex flats stacked into a vertical neighbourhood along internal streets. Then set it beside Park Hill in Sheffield (1961), Habitat 67 in Montréal (1967) with its stacked prefabricated modules, Denys Lasdun's Royal National Theatre in London (1976) and the Barbican Estate (1982), a walkway city of homes, an arts centre and a lake raised above street level.

Where can I see brutalist buildings?

The style spread to regions around the world, so there is brutalism on every continent. London has the Barbican, Trellick Tower and the Royal National Theatre; Boston has its City Hall; Le Corbusier's Capitol Complex stands in Chandigarh, India. Further afield are the Kenyatta International Convention Centre in Nairobi, the Bank of London and South America in Buenos Aires and the Museum of Modern Art in Rio de Janeiro.
