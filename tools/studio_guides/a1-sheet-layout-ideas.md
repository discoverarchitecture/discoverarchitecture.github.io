There is no single correct A1 layout, but there are a handful of arrangements that keep coming back because they work. Each one below suits a particular kind of project and a particular kind of drawing. Pick the one that matches the story you want to tell, then set it on a grid. Describe each layout to yourself as boxes first; the drawings come later.

## How to choose a layout

Before you look at the options, answer two questions. What is your strongest drawing? And what kind of story is your project: one big idea seen at a glance, a journey through a sequence of spaces, or an argument built from analysis? The strongest drawing usually becomes the hero, and the kind of story tells you how to arrange the rest.

All of these layouts assume a margin, a column grid and a reading order from top left to bottom right. If those are new to you, start with [how to lay out an A1 board](guide:how-to-lay-out-an-a1-architecture-board) and come back.

## 1. Hero and strip

One large image fills the top half to two-thirds of the sheet. Below it, a row of plans and sections in equal boxes runs across the full width, with captions underneath.

**Works when** you have one image that carries the whole idea, such as a perspective of the main space or a strong exterior view. It is the most forgiving layout and reads in seconds.

**Fails when** the hero is weak. A mediocre render at full size is louder than a good one at half size. If you are not sure about your hero, try another layout.

## 2. The left spine

A narrow column down the left side, one-quarter to one-third of the width, holds the title, a short statement, a site plan and a few diagrams. The rest of the sheet holds the main drawings at a large size.

**Works when** your project needs a little explaining before the drawings make sense: a complicated site, a research-led brief, or a concept that needs one or two diagrams. The spine reads first, like an introduction.

**Fails when** the spine fills up with paragraphs. Keep it to a few short lines and small images, or it turns into a report pinned to the wall.

## 3. The section band

A long section runs across the full width of the sheet, roughly through the middle. Plans sit above it, and perspectives or details below.

**Works when** the section is the idea: a building on a slope, a sequence of levels, light coming down through the floors, or a relationship between inside and outside ground. It works especially well in landscape.

**Fails when** the section is short or simple. Stretching a modest section across 800 mm just shows empty ground and sky.

## 4. The grid of equals

The sheet is divided into equal boxes, and each holds one diagram, photograph or study at the same size.

**Works when** the work is analytical: site analysis, a precedent study, material tests, or a series of design options you want the jury to compare side by side. Equal sizes say "compare these".

**Fails when** you use it for a finished design. Without a hero, the jury can't tell what matters most. If you need one board of analysis in a set, pair it with a board that has a clear hero.

## 5. The sequence

A row of frames, such as perspectives, sketches or model photos, runs left to right, each one a step along a route through the building. A plan nearby shows the route with numbered points that match the frames.

**Works when** the experience of moving through the project is the idea: arrival, compression and release, a route through a garden, a climb to a view.

**Fails when** the frames don't connect. Number them and match the numbers on the plan, or the jury won't know where each view was taken.

## 6. The exploded centre

An axonometric, exploded or cut-away, sits in the middle of the sheet. Details, material notes and small diagrams sit around it, linked with thin leader lines or numbers.

**Works when** the project is about how it is made: structure, assembly, a kit of parts, layers of a wall or roof. It suits technology and construction studios well.

**Fails when** the leader lines become a tangle. Keep them straight, thin and at consistent angles, or use numbered tags instead of lines.

## 7. The plan-led board

A large plan at a stated scale dominates the sheet. Sections and elevations are arranged around it, ideally aligned with the plan so that cut lines line up with the drawings they produce.

**Works when** the plan is the strongest drawing, such as housing layouts, a courtyard building or a masterplan, or when the brief asks for drawings at a set scale that the jury will measure.

**Fails when** the plan is printed so large that it's mostly empty rooms. Show furniture, people or landscape sparingly so the spaces read as places, and keep the line weights clear (see [presenting plans and sections](guide:how-to-present-architectural-plans-and-sections)).

## 8. The multi-board strip

Two or three boards are pinned side by side and designed as one long sheet. A horizon line, a ground line or a long section carries across all of them.

**Works when** the brief asks for more than one board. Designed together, they read as one story rather than three separate posters.

**Fails when** each board has a different grid. Use the same margins, the same column widths and the same title position on every board, or the strip looks like three different projects.

## Making any layout look finished

Whichever arrangement you choose, the same few habits make the difference:

- **One margin, one grid,** applied to every board in the set.
- **One type family,** with captions in the same position relative to their drawings every time.
- **One line-weight system** across all plans and sections, so the drawings look like a family.
- **One accent colour,** used for one meaning only, if you use colour at all.
- **Empty space on purpose,** next to the hero rather than scattered in thin gaps.

## Try two layouts, not one

When you are unsure, sketch the same content in two layouts on paper, side by side, small. Within a minute one will look obviously clearer. That is faster than building both on screen, and far faster than discovering the problem when the plot comes out.

## FAQ

### What is the best layout for an A1 architecture sheet?

The one that matches your story and your strongest drawing. Hero and strip suits a project with one strong image; a section band suits projects about levels or light; a grid of equals suits analysis and precedent studies.

### Should all my boards use the same layout?

They should share the same margins, grid, title position and type, so they read as a set. The arrangement of drawings within that grid can change from board to board.

### Can I use a template for my A1 board?

Yes, as long as you adjust it to your project. A template's boxes rarely match your drawings, so expect to change the grid, the hero size and the reading order.
