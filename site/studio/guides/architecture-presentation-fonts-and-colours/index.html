Fonts and colours for architecture presentation boards: a simple system that reads

The safest system for an architecture board is one plain sans-serif family in two weights, about four text sizes that are clearly different from each other, and a small palette: near-black for text and lines, greys, the colours of your real materials, and one accent colour with one meaning. That is enough to make a board look designed. The rest is testing: print part of the board at full size before the final plot, and check the colours on paper, not on screen.

Why a system beats a favourite font

Jurors don't mark the typeface. They notice when text gets in the way: a title in one font, captions in another, labels in a third, sizes that change from drawing to drawing. A board with a simple, consistent system reads as calm and finished even when the fonts are ordinary. A board with five fonts reads as unfinished even when each one is beautiful.

So decide the system once, at the start, and set it up as styles in your layout software (InDesign paragraph styles, or saved text styles in Illustrator or PowerPoint). Then every caption on every sheet is the same without you having to remember.

Choose one sans-serif family

A sans-serif with several weights covers everything a board needs: a heavy weight for the title, regular for text, and perhaps italic for captions. Plain grotesque and geometric families are the usual choice in architecture because they stay out of the way of the drawings.

Fonts you probably already have:

  • On a Mac: Helvetica, Helvetica Neue, Futura, Avenir and Gill Sans come with macOS.
  • On Windows: Arial, Calibri, Segoe UI and Bahnschrift (a DIN-style family) come with Windows 10 and 11.

Free fonts from Google Fonts, under open licences that allow print and commercial use:

  • Inter and IBM Plex Sans: neutral, with many weights; good for small labels.
  • Work Sans and Source Sans 3: slightly softer, very readable in body text.
  • Jost: a geometric family in the spirit of Futura, if you want that look on a computer without Futura.

Check the licence of anything else you download, and remember that the plotting computer may not have your font. Export the board as a PDF with fonts embedded or converted to outlines; our guide to sheet sizes and export covers the steps.

When a serif works

Serif fonts aren't wrong on a board. A serif for the title, or for a longer written statement, can suit a project about history, craft or a particular place, with a sans-serif for everything else. Keep it to that one pairing. What rarely works is a novelty font: a stencil for the title, or a "handwriting" font next to your real sketches, which looks like a costume beside the genuine thing.

Four sizes, two weights

Most boards need only four kinds of text:

  1. Title: the project name, large enough to read from across the room. On A1 that is often 60 pt or more.
  2. Statement and headings: the idea in a sentence or two, and the names of parts of the board, around 20 to 30 pt.
  3. Body text: short paragraphs of explanation, around 14 to 18 pt on A1.
  4. Captions and labels: drawing titles, room names, notes, no smaller than about 10 pt.

These are starting points; typefaces of the same point size look different sizes, so test them (below). Make each level clearly bigger than the one under it. Sizes that are close, like 12 and 14 pt, look like a mistake rather than a hierarchy.

Use two weights, regular and bold (or semibold). Bold picks out the title, headings and the first words of a caption; everything else is regular. Small labels set in capitals with a little extra letter spacing are a classic architectural touch, and they stay readable small. Keep long text in normal upper and lower case.

For body text, keep lines to roughly 50 to 75 characters and give the lines some space (line spacing of about 120 to 145% of the size). A paragraph the full width of an A1 sheet is very hard to read standing up; see laying out an A1 board for where text should sit.

Build the palette from the project

Start with the colours you need for drawing, not decoration:

  • Ink: a near-black for text and the main lines. A very dark grey often looks softer than pure black and prints just as well.
  • Greys: two or three, for things beyond the cut, context buildings and secondary lines. Very pale greys can disappear on the plot, so test them.
  • Material colours: the real colours of the building's main materials, used flat and softly in plans, sections and axonometrics, such as brick, timber, concrete or planting. These connect the drawings to the renders and photos.
  • One accent: a single strong colour with one meaning that the jury can follow across the board: the public route, the new intervention, the main idea. Our guide to plans and sections shows how.

Pick colours from your own renders or model photos with the eyedropper, so the drawings and the images belong together. If your renders are warm and hazy, a cold, saturated blue accent will look borrowed from another project.

Background: white, off-white or dark

White or a very slightly warm off-white is the safe background for a printed board. It lets line drawings read and costs nothing in ink. A dark board can look striking, but it uses a lot of ink, makes thin lines and small text harder to read, and shows every flaw in the plotting.

Whatever you choose, use it on every sheet. Mixing a white sheet with a black one in the same set breaks it into two projects.

Check it on paper and for colour-blind readers

Screens show colour in light (RGB); plotters print it in ink (CMYK and more). Bright screen colours, especially vivid blues, greens and oranges, often print duller, and pale tints can vanish. Before the final plot:

  • Print a test strip: a slice of the board with your text sizes, greys and colours, at 100% scale, ideally on the same plotter and paper you will use. On A3 at 100% you see a quarter of an A1 at its real size.
  • Stand back: look at it from two or three metres. If a caption or label can't be read, make it bigger, not bolder.
  • Look at it in greyscale: convert the board to greyscale on screen. If the accent and the material colours merge into the same grey, people with colour vision deficiency (about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women) will see them merge too. Change the lightness, not just the hue, and never use red against green as the only difference.

Carry the system into the deck

Your jury deck should use the same fonts, palette and accent as the board, so the two read as one project. Slides are seen on a projector or screen, which changes three things: thin font weights and pale greys can vanish, so use regular or heavier weights; contrast matters more, and the web accessibility standard's 4.5 : 1 contrast ratio for normal text is a useful target; and the text sizes are relative to the slide, not the paper. A quick checklist

  • One font family (two at most), set up as styles.
  • Four text sizes, each clearly different; captions no smaller than about 10 pt on A1.
  • Two weights; spaced capitals only for short labels.
  • Ink, a few greys, material colours, one accent with one meaning.
  • The same background on every sheet.
  • A test strip printed at full size, and a greyscale check.
  • The same system in the deck.

Questions students ask

What is the best font for an architecture presentation board?

There isn't one best font, but a plain sans-serif family with several weights is the safest choice: Helvetica, Futura or Avenir on a Mac, Arial or Bahnschrift on Windows, or free fonts such as Inter and IBM Plex Sans. Using one family consistently matters more than which one you pick.

How many colours should an architecture board have?

Keep it small: near-black ink, two or three greys, the colours of your main materials, and one accent colour with one clear meaning. If you can't say what a colour means, take it out.

What font size should text be on an A1 board?

As a starting point on A1, body text around 14 to 18 pt, captions and labels no smaller than about 10 pt, and a title large enough to read across the room. Typefaces differ, so print a slice of the board at full size and check it from a couple of metres away.
