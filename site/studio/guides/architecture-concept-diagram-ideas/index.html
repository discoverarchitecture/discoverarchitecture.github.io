Architecture concept diagram ideas: 10 diagrams juries understand at a glance

A concept diagram is a drawing with one job: to show a single idea about your project so clearly that a juror gets it before you speak. It leaves out almost everything a plan or section must include. The diagrams below are the kinds that come up again and again on good boards. Most projects need two or three of them, chosen for what the project is actually about, not one of each.

What makes a diagram a concept diagram

A plan shows everything at one floor. A diagram shows one thing across the whole project: the route, the light, the public edge, the structure. That is why it can be read in seconds, and why it is usually the first thing a jury looks at after the hero image.

The test is simple. Cover the label and ask a friend what the diagram shows. If they say the idea in their own words, it works. If they describe the building ("it's a sort of L-shape with a roof"), the diagram is still a drawing, and needs more taken out.

1. From a source to a form

Show where the idea came from and how it became architecture, in three or four steps joined by arrows: an object, a material, a ritual or a landscape, then the abstraction, then the built form. It answers "why does it look like that?" before anyone asks.

Watch out for literal translations. The best source diagrams show a quality you borrowed (lightness, layering, shade), not a building shaped like the object.

2. Massing moves

A row of small axonometrics showing how the form was made: the volume the site allows, then each move in turn (cut, lift, push, split, rotate), each with a two- or three-word label. Draw them from the same angle and at the same scale so only the move changes between frames. Our jury presentation ideas cover how to present a sequence like this.

3. The exploded axonometric

Pull the building apart vertically, layer by layer: ground and landscape, floors, structure, roof. Dashed lines between the layers show how they line up. It explains how a building is put together better than any number of plans, and it is the natural home for labels about construction and material.

Keep it to four or five layers. An explosion of every component becomes a parts catalogue.

4. Circulation

One line, or a few, showing how people move: the main route from the street, the stair, the loop through the galleries, the service route kept apart. Draw them over a faded plan or axonometric, with the line in your one accent colour and arrows only where the direction matters. Different users can get different line types (solid for visitors, dashed for staff), explained in a small key.

Suits museums, schools, hospitals, stations, markets: any project where the order of spaces is the design.

5. Programme and zoning

Blocks or tinted areas showing what goes where: public, shared and private; served and servant; day and night. Start from the brief's list of spaces and show how you grouped them. In section, zoning diagrams show stacking, such as noisy spaces low and quiet ones high.

Use a small range of tints of one or two colours, not a rainbow. If you need more than five categories, the diagram is trying to be a plan.

6. Light, air and climate

Arrows in section showing sun angles in summer and winter, daylight coming through a roof light, hot air rising out of a stack, a breeze crossing a courtyard. These are the diagrams jurors most often ask for when they are missing, because they show the building working rather than just standing there.

Base them on something real: the site's latitude for sun angles, the prevailing wind direction from a weather source, or an analysis you ran. A climate diagram that contradicts the site's actual sun path is worse than none.

7. Public to private

A gradient from the street to the most private room: a diagram in plan or section where the tone deepens as spaces become more protected. It shows thresholds, the edges where one kind of space hands over to the next, and it makes a strong argument for projects that open up to their neighbourhood.

8. Figure-ground and context

Buildings in solid black, open space in white, at the scale of the neighbourhood, with your project in your accent colour. It tells the jury in one glance how the project fits the grain of the city: whether it continues a street wall, opens a square or sits as an object in space. Two versions side by side (before and after) make the argument stronger.

9. Structure and grid

The columns, walls and spans on their own, drawn in plan or in an axonometric without the skin. It shows the order behind the building, explains why rooms are the sizes they are, and prepares the jury for questions about how it stands up.

Suits projects where the structure is the idea (a long span, a timber frame, a repeated module) and any project where the plan looks irregular but rests on a strict grid underneath.

10. Views and axes

Lines or cones from key points to what they look at: the view from the entrance to the courtyard, from the reading room to the old tree, the axis that ties the building to a monument. They justify openings and orientations that might otherwise look arbitrary.

Rules for drawing them clearly

  • One idea per diagram. If the label needs "and", it is two diagrams.
  • Same view, same scale across a series, so only the idea changes from frame to frame.
  • Strip everything else. No textures, no furniture, no trees unless they are the point. Faded context in light grey; the idea in black or your accent colour.
  • One accent colour, used the same way on every diagram and ideally the same one used on your plans. The guide to presenting plans and sections explains why one is enough.
  • Arrows mean one thing. If arrows show movement in one diagram and wind in the next, give them different weights or styles.
  • Labels of two to five words, set in the same type as the rest of the board.
  • Readable from a step back. Diagrams are often printed small; check them in your A3 test print.

Where diagrams go on the board

Put each diagram next to the drawing it explains: the circulation diagram beside the plan, the light diagram beside the section. The source-to-form and massing diagrams usually sit near the title and the project statement, because they introduce the idea. A left spine or a section band layout gives them a natural place.

Questions students ask

How many concept diagrams should an architecture board have?

Usually two to four, each showing a different idea. One strong source or massing diagram near the title, and one or two analytical diagrams (circulation, light, zoning) next to the plans and sections, cover most projects.

Should concept diagrams be hand-drawn or digital?

Either works. Hand-drawn diagrams on trace often show thinking more honestly; digital ones from your model are precise and easy to keep consistent. What matters is that each one shows a single idea clearly and that a series shares the same view and style.

What is the difference between a concept diagram and a sketch?

A sketch explores; it can be loose and show many things at once. A concept diagram explains one finished idea to someone else, so it is simplified, labelled and drawn to be read in seconds.
