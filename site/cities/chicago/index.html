Architecture in Chicago

43 buildings

Chicago is where the skyscraper was worked out, and it holds one of the world's largest and most varied collections. The Great Chicago Fire of 1871 destroyed most of downtown, and the city rebuilt. The Montauk Building (1882–83) was the first to be called a skyscraper, and Jenney's Home Insurance Building (1885) is considered the first with steel in its structural frame. A steel skeleton meant height was no longer limited by the strength of the walls. Louis Sullivan set aside historical precedent to stress height, and from 1963 a "Second Chicago School" grew from Mies van der Rohe's work at IIT.

The Monadnock Building (1893) is the tallest load-bearing masonry building ever built. The Reliance Building (1895) was the first skyscraper with large plate-glass windows over most of its surface. Sullivan's Sullivan Center (Carson Pirie Scott Building), finished in 1907, sets a white terracotta grid over an ornate cast-iron base. Mies's 860-880 Lake Shore Drive (1951) places two steel and glass towers at right angles, their I-beam mullions expressing the frame. Bruce Graham and Fazlur Khan's John Hancock Center (1969) tapers inside an X-braced tube that resists the wind.
