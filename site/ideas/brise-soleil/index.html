Brise-soleil in architecture

62 buildings

A brise-soleil, French for "sun breaker", is a screen that shades a facade and cuts a building's heat gain by deflecting sunlight. The simplest form is a horizontal projection on the sunny side. It is used most on glass facades that would otherwise overheat, and it still lets in the low sun of mornings, evenings and winter. Le Corbusier popularised patterned concrete versions, and Santiago Calatrava designed a moving one shaped like a wing. At the Institut du Monde Arabe, 240 photo-sensitive, motor-controlled apertures act as a brise-soleil.

Read moreShow less

Lúcio Costa and Oscar Niemeyer's Gustavo Capanema Palace in Rio de Janeiro (1945) sets adjustable brise-soleil on the sun facade of an office slab. Le Corbusier's Palace of Assembly in Chandigarh (1962) is a parliament built for the Punjab sun. His Curutchet House in La Plata (1953) fits house and clinic on a narrow lot behind a brise-soleil. Calatrava's Milwaukee Art Museum, Quadracci Pavilion (2001) opens and folds a winged sunscreen of steel fins above a glass hall. Renzo Piano's New York Times Building (2007) screens clear glass walls with horizontal ceramic rods that shade the offices.
