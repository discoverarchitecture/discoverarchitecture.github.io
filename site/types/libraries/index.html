Library architecture

132 buildings

Library architecture has to store books and bring people to them, and over time the balance has moved from archive to public room. The first libraries were archives of the earliest writing, such as the Library of Ashurbanipal in Mesopotamia. As collections grew, libraries ran short of space and introduced compact shelving on tracks. Between 1883 and 1929, 1,689 Carnegie libraries were built in the United States, and the public libraries of the 1960s were shaped by modernism. Today libraries also work as community hubs.

Henri Labrouste's Bibliothèque Sainte-Geneviève in Paris, opened in 1851, leaves its slender cast-iron columns and arches visible over the reading room; it is called a major step towards modern architecture, and it influenced the Boston Public Library, McKim Building (1895). Gordon Bunshaft's Beinecke Rare Book & Manuscript Library in New Haven (1963) filters light through translucent marble panels onto a glass-walled book tower. Toyo Ito's Sendai Mediatheque (2001) carries thin floors on thirteen lattice tubes. OMA's Seattle Central Library (2004) stacks shifted platforms inside a diamond-mesh skin, and its Books Spiral keeps the nonfiction collection in one continuous run.
