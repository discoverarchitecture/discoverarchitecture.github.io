An architecture project statement is a short text, usually 100 to 200 words, that tells a stranger what your project is and why it takes the form it does. It should say four things in order: the problem or condition the project answers, the site and programme in one line, the idea, and two or three moves in the building that carry that idea out. Write it in plain, present-tense sentences, cut anything the drawings can't back up, then shorten it for where it will be read: a jury board, a portfolio page or a competition entry.

## What a project statement is for

The statement speaks for your project when you aren't there: a juror walking the pin-up before you talk, an admissions tutor opening your portfolio, a competition panel reading entries with no names on them. The words decide whether the reader looks closely at the drawings or moves on.

It is not the same as your concept. The concept is the idea itself, ideally one sentence with "because" in it; our guide to [developing an architecture concept](guide:how-to-develop-an-architecture-concept) is about finding it. The statement is that sentence plus the case for it: where it came from and where it shows in the building. Without the concept, no amount of good writing will make the statement clear.

## What it must say

Four things, in this order, each in one or two sentences.

1. **The problem or condition.** What is difficult, missing or unusual about this place or brief? It gives the reader a reason to care in the first line.
2. **The site and programme, in one line.** What the building is and where it stands.
3. **The idea.** Your concept sentence, said plainly: because of this condition, the building does this.
4. **How the building answers it.** Two or three specific moves, in plan, section or material, that a reader can then find in the drawings. This turns an idea into a project.

## A structure that works: five sentences

If you are stuck, fill in five sentences:

- **Sentence 1, the condition** (15 to 25 words): what you found on the site or in the brief.
- **Sentence 2, the project** (15 to 25 words): building type, programme and site.
- **Sentence 3, the idea** (15 to 30 words): the response, with "because" or "so" in it.
- **Sentences 4 and 5, the moves** (40 to 80 words together): the plan, section or material decisions that follow from the idea.

That gives roughly 100 to 180 words. Once it reads well you can change the order, for example to open with the idea, but never drop one of the four parts.

## Tone and voice

- **Present tense, active verbs.** "The roof shades the court", not "the roof was designed to provide shading to the court".
- **Concrete nouns.** A reader can picture a stair or a window seat; they can't picture "an experience".
- **One claim per sentence.** If a sentence has three commas and an "and", split it.
- **Plain words over studio words.** "Dialogue", "liminal", "juxtaposition", "fluid", "permeable", "seamless" and "a sense of place" could describe any building. Say what you mean in this project, or cut them.
- **No claim the drawings can't show.** "Sustainable" needs a how, such as cross-ventilation in a section you have drawn.

Read it aloud. Where you stumble, the sentence is too long or too vague.

## What to cut

- **The brief, restated.** The jury wrote it; a portfolio reader needs one clause.
- **History that didn't shape the design.** Keep one fact about the site's past only if it changed the building.
- **The room list.** The plan shows the rooms. Name only those the idea depends on.
- **The process diary.** "First I studied the site, then I made three models" belongs in the process drawings.
- **Adjectives doing the work.** "Iconic", "unique", "vibrant", "dynamic": show it, don't say it.
- **Quotes from famous architects.** They say nothing about your building.

## A worked example: before and after

The project below is made up for this guide, and so are its facts.

### Before

*This project explores the dialogue between community and the everyday through a fluid and permeable architecture. Situated in a vibrant urban context, the design seeks to create a sense of place and belonging for all users. Inspired by the idea of flow, the building features a dynamic form that blurs the boundaries between inside and outside. Sustainable materials and passive strategies are used throughout. The ground floor contains a laundry, toilets, a store and a reception, and the first floor contains a workshop, a meeting room and a kitchen. The project aims to become a landmark for the community.*

Almost none of it is about this building. There is no site, the idea is a word ("flow") rather than a reason, the programme is a room list, and "sustainable" and "landmark" are claims nothing on the board supports. Swap the room list and it could describe a library.

### After

*On a 1970s housing estate, 400 flats share one lift lobby and no other indoor room. The project is a launderette and repair workshop on the empty corner plot beside the bus stop. Most of the time in a launderette is spent waiting, so the building is designed around the wait. The machines line the back wall; the rest of the ground floor is one public room, with a long table and a window seat facing the street. A deep timber canopy shelters the drying racks and the bus queue together. Above, a workshop for mending clothes, bikes and furniture is reached by an outside stair, so it can stay open after the laundry closes.*

This one is 115 words. Sentence 1 is the condition, sentence 2 the site and programme, sentence 3 the idea, and the last three are moves, each one a drawing the jury can find: the plan with the long table, the section through the canopy, the outside stair in the elevation.

## Three versions: board, portfolio and competition

Write the full statement once, then cut it for each place it goes.

### On a jury board

Put it beside or under the hero image, where the eye lands first, in text large enough to read standing at the wall, and keep it to around 100 to 150 words. Many boards also carry a one-line subtitle under the title: your idea in under 20 words, which the statement expands. Our [A1 board layout guide](guide:how-to-lay-out-an-a1-architecture-board) covers where the words sit on the sheet.

### In a portfolio

The reader didn't set the brief and won't hear you talk. Each project's opening page needs a shorter statement, often 40 to 80 words, with the brief in a clause, plus the plain facts: type, place, year of study, solo or group work. The [portfolio guide](guide:how-to-make-an-architecture-portfolio) covers the rest of the page.

### For a competition

Follow the word limit exactly and answer the question the competition asks, in its own words where you can. If it is anonymous, as many are, leave out your name, your school and anything else that identifies you. Panels read many entries, so put the idea first. The made-up example above, cut to 50 words for an imaginary competition on shared space in housing:

*Most of the time in a launderette is spent waiting, so this one is designed around the wait. On the empty corner of a 1970s estate, the machines line the back wall and the rest is a public room facing the street, under a canopy shared with the bus stop.*

## Common mistakes

- **Writing it last, at 4 a.m.** Draft it while you design; it shows you where the project is vague.
- **Words and drawings that disagree.** The statement says three courtyards; the plan shows two.
- **Too long for the board.** A 400-word block in small type is skipped. Cut it, don't shrink it.
- **Opening with a dictionary definition.** It wastes the first line.
- **Every sentence starting "The project...".** Vary the subject: the roof, the court, the stair.
- **Not proofread.** Ask a classmate to read it cold and tell you the idea back; if they can't, rewrite sentence 3.

## FAQ

### How long should an architecture project statement be?

On a jury board, around 100 to 150 words is usually enough; up to about 200 if the board is large and the project complex. A portfolio project page needs less, often 40 to 80 words. For a competition, the brief's word limit is the rule, and going over can disqualify an entry.

### Should I write a project statement in the first person?

On a board or a competition entry, write about the building, in the present tense: "the roof shades the court". In a portfolio, the first person is fine for what you did, especially in group work ("I drew the sections and built the model"). Whichever you choose, don't switch halfway through.

### Should the statement mention the site and climate?

Yes, but only the conditions that shaped the design, in a sentence. If the sun, a slope or a busy road decided where the rooms go, say so and point to the drawing that shows it. Site facts that changed nothing belong on the site analysis drawings, if anywhere.
