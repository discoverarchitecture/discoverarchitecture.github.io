Good site analysis on a board is not everything you found out about the site. It is the three to five findings that changed your design, drawn clearly on one consistent base map, each one paired with the move it led to. A jury should be able to point at any part of your building and find the reason for it in the analysis.

## Analysis is an argument, not an inventory

Students often present site analysis as a catalogue: location map, land use, traffic, sun path, wind rose, photographs, a SWOT table. Each sheet is accurate, and together they say nothing, because the jury can't tell which facts mattered.

Start from the other end. List the decisions in your design that come from the site: why the entrance is on that street, why the building steps down, why the courtyard faces that way, why the tree stays. Then present the analysis that explains those decisions, and leave the rest in your sketchbook. If you can't connect a map to a decision, it doesn't go on the board.

## What to look at on site

You won't present all of these, but you should have looked at all of them before deciding which ones matter:

- **Context and grain:** what surrounds the site, how big the buildings are, how close they stand, what the street wall is like.
- **Movement and access:** how people arrive on foot, by bike, by public transport and by car; where the busy edges and the quiet ones are; where service vehicles can get in.
- **Uses and activity:** what happens around the site and when. A street can be empty at nine in the morning and packed at nine at night.
- **Climate:** the sun's path through the year, the prevailing winds, rainfall, heat. Base it on real data for the location, not on a generic diagram.
- **Ground and nature:** slopes, levels, water, soil, existing trees worth keeping.
- **Noise, smell and views:** what the site hears and smells, what it looks at and what looks at it.
- **History and character:** what was there before, materials and building traditions nearby, anything protected.
- **Rules:** setbacks, height limits and other planning constraints, if your brief includes them.

Visit at more than one time of day if you can, and take photographs from where a person would stand, numbered against a key plan so you can find them later.

## The drawings that do the work

### One base map, used everywhere

Draw (or trace) one clean base map of the site and its surroundings, and use it for every analysis layer: same scale, same orientation, same north arrow, same line weights. Each map then shows only its layer, such as movement in your accent colour, over the same faded base. The jury learns the map once and reads every layer instantly.

If you trace from an online map, redraw it rather than pasting a screenshot. OpenStreetMap's data is free to use with the credit "© OpenStreetMap contributors"; commercial map screenshots usually aren't yours to print.

### Figure-ground

Buildings in black, open space in white. It shows the grain of the area at a glance and sets up the argument for how your building fits it. See [concept diagram ideas](guide:architecture-concept-diagram-ideas) for using it before and after.

### Movement and edges

Lines of different weights for busy and quiet routes, arrows for the main arrivals, a dashed line for service access. Mark the edges of the site by character: active street, quiet lane, garden, blank wall. This map usually explains your entrance and your plan's orientation.

### Sun and wind

A sun-path diagram for the site's latitude, or simpler, the sun's angles in section at midsummer and midwinter. Add the prevailing wind direction as arrows. Free tools can do the work: weather files for most cities are freely available, and tools such as Ladybug for Grasshopper or Climate Consultant turn them into sun paths and wind roses. Then say in one sentence what the data means for the design ("low winter sun from the south, so the courtyard opens south").

### A section through the site and the street

Site analysis is often drawn only in plan. One long section through the street, the site and its neighbours shows level changes, building heights, shade and the relationship to the street better than any map.

### Photographs, with a key

A strip of three to six photographs, each numbered on a small key plan. Choose ones that show the findings: the busy edge, the old tree, the blank wall next door, the view you want to keep.

### Activity over time

If the life of the place matters (markets, schools, stations, public spaces), map it at two or three times of day. It is often the most original part of an analysis because nobody else has it; you have to stand there and watch.

## From finding to design move

The strongest way to present analysis is as pairs: a finding, then the response.

- *The main road is busy all day, the back lane quiet* → the shops face the road and the quiet rooms face the lane.
- *Afternoon sun is harsh from the west* → the west side is shaded by a deep roof overhang.
- *Two large trees stand near the middle of the site* → the courtyard is planned around them.

These are made-up examples; yours come from your own site. Lay them out as a short row on the board, or a slide each in the deck. When a juror asks "why is the entrance there?", you point at the pair.

## How much space it gets

- **On a design jury board:** usually a column or a band, roughly a fifth to a third of the sheet, often down the left side or across the top so it reads first. Our [layout ideas](guide:a1-sheet-layout-ideas) show where a "left spine" of analysis fits.
- **On a thesis or urban project:** often a whole board of its own, because the analysis is part of the argument.
- **In a jury deck:** two or three slides after the idea and before the plans. The [10–15 slide deck guide](guide:architecture-jury-presentation-slides) places them.

## Common mistakes

- **Too many maps, too small.** Five clear layers beat twelve postage stamps.
- **Generic diagrams.** A sun path that isn't for your latitude, or a wind rose copied from another city, will be noticed.
- **Inconsistent maps.** Different scales, orientations and styles make the jury relearn every map.
- **SWOT tables with nothing behind them.** If you use one, every line should link to something you drew.
- **Analysis that stops.** If the plans never refer back to the findings, the analysis looks like homework done for its own sake.

## FAQ

### What should be included in an architecture site analysis?

Look at context, movement and access, uses and activity, climate, ground and trees, noise and views, history and the planning rules. Then present only the three to five findings that shaped your design, each on the same base map and paired with the design move it led to.

### How do you present site analysis at a jury?

Briefly and as an argument. Show the site in context first, then the few findings that matter, each with its design response, before moving on to the plans. Two or three minutes, or two or three slides, is usually enough for a design jury.

### Can I use Google Maps images in my site analysis?

It is safer to redraw your base map. Map screenshots are usually covered by the provider's terms. OpenStreetMap data is free to use with the credit "© OpenStreetMap contributors", and a redrawn base map also lets you control what it shows.
