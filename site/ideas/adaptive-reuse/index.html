Adaptive reuse in architecture

Questions students ask

What is adaptive reuse in architecture?

Adaptive reuse means putting an existing building to a new use rather than demolishing it. This saves the expense of demolition, retains the energy already invested in the original structure and preserves a community's connection to its past. Old buildings can also supply materials to new projects. Tate Modern in London, a gallery in a power station that opened in 2000, is a classic example.

What are good examples of adaptive reuse?

Good examples include Paris's Musée d'Orsay (1986), set inside a former railway station, and the SESC Pompéia (1986) in São Paulo by Lina Bo Bardi, which retains a drum factory and uses it as a leisure centre. Tate Modern's Turbine Hall has become an indoor street; New York's High Line (2009) is a park laid along an old elevated railway; and Hamburg's Elbphilharmonie (2017) puts a glass crown on top of a brick warehouse.

What are the challenges of adaptive reuse?

The main challenges are the existing structure and building codes, which is why a condition survey determines whether a project is viable. Designers then must choose what to keep and where a new opening can be cut into the old structure. At Zeitz MOCAA (2017) in Cape Town, a grain silo is carved into an atrium in the shape of a single corn kernel, and Peter Zumthor's Kolumba Museum (2007) builds a brick veil over a ruined Gothic church.

How do architects add new parts to old buildings?

Compare three approaches. Carlo Scarpa's Castelvecchio Museum in Verona (1975) reworks a medieval castle with precise new joints, cuts and display stands. OMA's Fondazione Prada in Milan (2015) joins former distillery buildings with new galleries and a tower. Zaha Hadid Architects' Port House in Antwerp (2016) hovers a faceted glass extension, shaped like a ship's bow, on concrete piers above an old fire station.
