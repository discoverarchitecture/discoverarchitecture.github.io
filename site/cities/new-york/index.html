Architecture in New York

48 buildings

New York's architecture is worth studying for its skyscrapers: the city has one of the largest and most varied collections in the world. The 1916 Zoning Resolution required setbacks and limited towers to a share of the lot; Art Deco towers such as the Chrysler and Empire State buildings took their tapered tops from those rules. Grand Central Terminal was built in the Beaux-Arts style. Later, the Seagram Building and 330 West 42nd Street became early influential examples of the International Style in the United States.

Cass Gilbert's Woolworth Building (1913) dresses a steel frame in Gothic Revival terracotta. William Van Alen's Chrysler Building (1930), which many historians and architects rank among the city's finest, stacks a stainless-steel crown of sunburst arches above its setbacks. The Empire State Building (1931) was the first building to pass 100 storeys and the world's tallest until 1970. Mies van der Rohe's Seagram Building (1958) stands back behind a plaza, its bronze mullions expressing the frame; the critic Paul Goldberger called it one of the city's most copied. The MetLife Building (1963) sets a precast concrete slab across Park Avenue above Grand Central.
