A site analysis is the study you make of a site before you design on it: what surrounds it, how people reach it, how sun, wind and noise act on it, how the ground moves, what was there before and what the rules allow. You gather it by visiting, reading maps, taking photographs and drawing sections through the site. The aim is not a thick file of facts. It is the three to five findings that tell you where the building goes, which way it faces and what it must keep.

## What a site analysis covers

Most studio briefs expect the same ten topics. Look at all of them; you only know which ones matter once you have looked.

- **Context.** What lies within a few minutes' walk: uses, landmarks, open spaces.
- **Access and movement.** How people arrive on foot, by bike, by public transport and by car; where service vehicles get in; which edges are busy or quiet.
- **Sun path and climate.** Where the sun is through the day and the year; temperature, rainfall and humidity. Outside the tropics, the midday sun is to the south in the northern hemisphere and to the north in the southern hemisphere.
- **Wind.** The prevailing winds from weather data, and local effects: gaps that funnel wind, gusty corners of tall blocks, sheltered pockets.
- **Views.** What the site looks at, what looks back, and which views to frame or screen.
- **Topography.** Levels, slopes, drainage, water, soil and trees worth keeping.
- **Noise.** Roads, railways, schools, markets, bars: what you hear, from which side and at what time.
- **Built fabric and scale.** Building heights, plot sizes, the street wall, materials and roof shapes nearby.
- **History.** What stood there before, how the area grew, anything listed or protected.
- **Regulations.** Setbacks, height limits, plot coverage and heritage controls, usually given in the brief; in practice they come from the local zoning code or planning policy.

## How to gather it

### Visit, and go more than once

Nothing replaces standing on the site. Take a printed base map, a pen and a phone. Walk the boundary, then the routes people use to reach it. If you can, go twice, say a weekday morning and an evening, because a street can change completely between them.

Mark the map as you go: noise, shade, where people gather, wind, views. Write the time beside each note. A note without a time is half a fact.

### Maps and aerial images

Work from one clean base map. A scale of 1:500 or 1:1000 usually suits the site itself, and 1:2500 or smaller the wider area. OpenStreetMap is free to use with the credit "© OpenStreetMap contributors", and many national mapping agencies publish open data too. Redraw what you need rather than printing screenshots of commercial maps, which are usually not yours to reuse.

Aerial images help with roof shapes, trees and the grain of the area. For history, the historical imagery in Google Earth Pro and old maps in a city archive or library show how the site changed.

### Photographs, numbered on a key plan

Photograph from where a person stands. Cover each edge, the views out, the neighbours and the details: paving, walls, the tree you want to keep. Number every photo and mark its position and direction on a key plan the same day; a hundred unlabelled photos are hard to use a week later.

### Sections through the site

Draw at least one section through the site and its neighbours, and if the ground slopes, one in each direction. Take levels from contours on a map or from the survey in your brief. A section shows what a plan hides: level changes, the height of the neighbours, how far their shade reaches, how the street meets the ground floor.

### Climate data

Don't draw a sun path or wind rose from memory. Weather files for most cities can be downloaded free, and tools such as Ladybug for Grasshopper or Climate Consultant turn them into sun paths and wind roses for your exact location. Keep only the charts that change the design.

## From facts to findings

You will now have far more than fits on a board. Sort it with one question: *what does this mean for the design?*

- *A busy road on one side, a quiet lane on the other* → the entrance and shopfronts face the road, the quiet rooms face the lane.
- *The ground falls three metres across the site* → the building steps down, with an entrance at each level.
- *The best view and the harshest afternoon sun come from the same side* → deep shading that still lets you see out.

These are made-up examples; yours come from your own site. A fact with no consequence stays in your sketchbook. What remains are your findings, usually three to five, and one of them is often the seed of the design idea.

## Turn the findings into a few diagrams

The board needs diagrams, not data. Five rules of thumb:

1. **One base map for every layer.** Same scale, orientation and faded base; only the layer changes.
2. **One idea per diagram.** If a diagram needs a long legend, it is doing too much.
3. **Draw the finding, not the data.** A wind rose is data. Arrows showing the breeze reaching the courtyard is a finding.
4. **Include a section.** A site section with the summer and winter sun angles drawn on it often explains more than three plans.
5. **End with the move.** The last diagram in the row shows the building's first form answering the findings: pulled back from the noise, opened to the view, shaded from the west.

For where analysis sits on the board, read [how to present site analysis](guide:how-to-present-site-analysis); for the drawing style, see [concept diagram ideas](guide:architecture-concept-diagram-ideas).

## What juries look for

Jurors rarely ask for more analysis. They ask *why*: why the entrance is there, why the building faces that way. A good analysis lets you answer by pointing at a drawing. They look for:

- **A clear line from site to design.** Each major move can be traced back to a finding.
- **Signs that you went there.** Your own photographs, notes on activity at different times, things no map could tell you.
- **Accuracy.** A north arrow, a scale, a sun path for the right latitude. Mistakes here make jurors doubt the rest.
- **Selection.** Five clear findings show judgement; fifteen thin maps suggest you didn't decide what mattered.
- **Honesty about trade-offs.** If the view and the sun pull in different directions, say how you chose.

There is more on this in [what architecture jurors look for](guide:what-architecture-jurors-look-for).

## Learn from buildings that answer their site

Look at how well-known buildings respond to their sites, and ask what the analysis must have found:

- [Salk Institute](/buildings/salk-institute/), La Jolla: the plaza between the laboratories opens to the Pacific, so the view of the ocean becomes the centre of the plan.
- [Magney House](/buildings/magney-house/), Bingie Point, Australia: a long house one room deep, with louvres along the north side, which is the sunny side in the southern hemisphere.
- [Oslo Opera House](/buildings/oslo-opera-house/): a marble roof that slopes into the fjord, so the building becomes a public place by the water.
- [Heritance Kandalama](/buildings/heritance-kandalama/), Sri Lanka: a hotel laid along a rock face rather than placed on top of it, and left to be covered by the jungle.
- [Kanchanjunga Apartments](/buildings/kanchanjunga-apartments/), Mumbai: two-storey garden verandas that open the flats to the sea view and breeze from the west while shading them from the afternoon sun and monsoon rain.

## FAQ

### What should a site analysis include in architecture?

Context, access and movement, sun path and climate, wind, views, topography, noise, built fabric and scale, history and regulations. Study all of them, then present only the three to five findings that shaped your design.

### Can I do a site analysis without visiting the site?

You can do part of it from maps, aerial images, street-level imagery and climate data, and for a site abroad that may be all you have. Say so on the board, and be careful with things you can only learn by being there, such as noise, activity at different times and how the place feels.

### How many diagrams should a site analysis have?

For a design studio board, usually four to six: a location or context map, movement, sun and wind, views or edges, a site section, and a final diagram of the design's response. A thesis or urban project often needs more, sometimes a whole board.
