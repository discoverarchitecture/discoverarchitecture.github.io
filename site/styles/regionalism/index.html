Critical regionalism in architecture

255 buildings

Critical regionalism is modern architecture rooted in its place: it starts from the local topography, climate and light rather than a universal style. The idea was first presented in 1981 by the theorists Alexander Tzonis and Liane Lefaivre, and the historian Kenneth Frampton developed it further. It tries to mediate between the global and the local languages of architecture, and it rejects the whimsy and ornament of postmodernism. It has grown into distinct sub-styles around the world.

Alvar Aalto's Säynätsalo Town Hall in Jyväskylä (1952) gathers brick rooms around a raised grass courtyard, with timber trusses over the council chamber. Álvaro Siza's Boa Nova Tea House in Leça da Palmeira (1963) sets low tiled roofs among the rocks above the sea, reached by steps through the boulders. Jørn Utzon's Bagsværd Church near Copenhagen (1976) hides a billowing concrete ceiling, washed with daylight, inside a plain industrial shell. Geoffrey Bawa's Parliament of Sri Lanka (1982) groups pavilions with stacked copper roofs on an island in an artificial lake. Balkrishna Doshi's Indian Institute of Management Bangalore (1983) links courts and classrooms of rough granite and concrete with long corridors under open pergolas.

Questions students ask

What is critical regionalism in architecture?

Critical regionalism is modern architecture grounded in a particular place, taking local topography, climate and light as its starting point instead of a universal style. It seeks a middle path between architecture's global and local languages, and it turns away from postmodernism's whimsy and ornament. Alexander Tzonis and Liane Lefaivre, both theorists, first put the idea forward in 1981; the historian Kenneth Frampton then took it further.

What are the key features of regionalist architecture?

Regionalist buildings answer their site, not a style, because Frampton stressed topography, climate and light. Siza's Boa Nova Tea House (1963) tucks low tiled roofs between rocks over the sea. Renzo Piano's Jean-Marie Tjibaou Cultural Centre in Nouméa (1998) sets ten curved timber-ribbed cases that recall Kanak huts, their double skins venting the trade winds, and Luis Barragán's house in Mexico City (1948) turns a blank wall to the street and works with colour and light.

Which regionalist buildings should architecture students study?

Bagsværd Church (1976) near Copenhagen, by Jørn Utzon, is one cited example: inside a plain industrial shell, a billowing concrete ceiling catches the daylight. Set it beside the Boa Nova Tea House (1963) and Leça Swimming Pools (1966) by Siza, the Parliament of Sri Lanka (1982) by Geoffrey Bawa, on its island in a lake, and the Indian Institute of Management Bangalore by Balkrishna Doshi (1983), whose corridors run beneath open pergolas.

Who are the main regionalist architects?

Its leading architects come from many countries: Jørn Utzon in Denmark, Álvaro Siza in Portugal, Luis Barragán in Mexico, Geoffrey Bawa in Sri Lanka, and Balkrishna Doshi and Charles Correa in India, whose Jawahar Kala Kendra in Jaipur (1991) is laid out on a nine-square plan. That spread follows from the way critical regionalism has developed into separate sub-styles across the world.
