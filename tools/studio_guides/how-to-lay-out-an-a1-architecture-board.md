Most of a good A1 layout is decided before you place a single drawing: orientation, margins, a column grid and a reading order. Get those four right and the drawings almost place themselves. Skip them and you spend the night nudging images a few millimetres left and right, and the board still looks restless.

## Start with the sheet: size and orientation

An A1 sheet is 594 × 841 mm. Before anything else, read the brief again. Some schools pin up portrait, some landscape, and some ask for two or three boards that read side by side as one strip. Many North American schools use 24 × 36 inch sheets (ARCH D) instead, which are close to A1 but a little wider in proportion, so a layout made for one won't drop straight into the other without adjustment ([sheet sizes compared](guide:architecture-sheet-sizes-a1-a0-24x36)).

Choose the orientation from your drawings, not from habit. A long site section, a linear building or a route through a landscape wants landscape. A tower, a tall section or a project that reads from the ground up can sit well in portrait. If you are pinning several boards in a row, portrait sheets side by side make a long horizontal band, which suits a story told from left to right.

## Margins: the frame that holds everything

Set the margin first. Around 20 to 25 mm on every side is a sensible start for A1. Some people leave a slightly deeper margin at the bottom so the content doesn't look as if it is sliding off the sheet. Everything goes inside the margin, including the title, your name and the project number.

The margin isn't wasted paper. When your board is pinned next to twenty others, a clean, even border is what makes it look finished. Ask your print shop about the plotter's unprintable edge too; some machines clip a few millimetres, and you don't want to find that out on the morning of the jury.

## Pick a column grid you will actually use

Divide the area inside the margins into columns, with a gutter (a small gap) between them. Two grids that work:

- **Landscape A1, six columns.** With 25 mm margins the usable width is 791 mm. Six columns with 10 mm gutters gives columns of about 123 mm. Six divides into halves, thirds and sixths, so you have many options without losing order.
- **Portrait A1, four columns.** With 25 mm margins the usable width is 544 mm. Four columns with 10 mm gutters gives columns of about 128 mm. Four divides into halves and quarters, which is plenty on a narrower sheet.

Then add rows. Divide the usable height into equal bands with the same gutter, and you have a modular grid: a set of boxes that every drawing, diagram and block of text snaps to. A drawing can span one module or six, but its edges land on grid lines. Not every module needs filling.

Most layout software (InDesign, Illustrator, Affinity Publisher, even PowerPoint with guides) can draw columns and rows for you. Set them once, lock them, and stop thinking about them.

## Decide the reading order before the sizes

A juror reads a board the way they read a page: top left to bottom right. Before you open the software, write down the order in which you would explain the project out loud. For most design studio projects it is something like idea, site, hero view, plans, sections, details, then process and models.

Now sketch the board as boxes on a scrap of paper, small enough that you can't draw details. Number the boxes in reading order. This takes five minutes, and it settles the arguments you would otherwise have with yourself at 2 a.m. The order decides where things go. Importance, in the next step, decides how big they are.

If you would rather start from a laid-out draft and adjust it, [Studio FGA](/studio/) places your own drawings on an A1 board that you can download and keep editing. Either way, the reading order should be yours.

## Size by importance: one hero, a few supporting, the rest small

Give one drawing the most space. This is the hero: the image that shows your idea best, usually a perspective, a strong section or an axonometric. As a rough guide it takes a quarter to a third of the usable area. Two to four supporting drawings take medium modules. Diagrams, details and process sketches go small.

If everything is the same size, the jury has to decide what matters, and they will decide differently from you. One clear hero beats three medium images competing.

Where your brief asks for drawings at a stated scale, place those first. A plan at 1:100 or 1:200 has a fixed size on the sheet, and everything else fits around it. A plan you have shrunk to fit the space is a plan nobody can measure, and a sharp juror will notice.

## Give text its own places

Decide where words live and keep them there: a title band along the top or down one column, a short statement of the idea near the hero, and a caption directly under or beside each drawing, aligned to the same edge.

Keep text blocks one or two columns wide, never the full width of the sheet; long lines are tiring to read standing up. For sizes, a reasonable starting point on A1 is a title that reads from across the room, body text around 14 to 18 pt, and captions no smaller than about 10 pt. Test them, though, because typefaces differ (see the A3 test below). Use one typeface family, two at most.

## Align edges, not centres

The quickest way to make a board look calm is to align edges. Images in a row share a top line. Images in a column share a left edge. Captions start on the same grid line as the drawing above them.

Crop drawings tightly to their content before placing them, so the edge you align is the drawing's edge rather than a box of empty white paper with the drawing floating somewhere inside it. For a plan with an irregular outline, align the plan's frame or its cut line, and keep the spacing around it the same as elsewhere.

## Leave one area empty on purpose

White space should be a decision, not whatever is left over. One or two empty modules next to the hero make the hero stronger. If the board feels crowded, take a drawing off rather than shrinking everything to fit. The drawing you remove can go in the [jury deck](guide:architecture-jury-presentation-slides).

## Test at A3, then check at full size

An A3 print is exactly half of A1 in each direction, so it shows the whole layout at a quarter of the area. Print it, pin it on a wall and step back about two metres. Ask three questions: what do I see first, does the order make sense without me explaining it, and can I still read the captions? Captions that are hard to read at A3 from close up are probably fine at A1; captions you can't read at all are too small.

If you can, also print one strip of the board at full size, or zoom to 100% on screen, to check line weights. Thin lines that look crisp on a monitor can disappear on a plot. For more on that, see [how to present plans and sections](guide:how-to-present-architectural-plans-and-sections).

## FAQ

### Should an A1 architecture board be portrait or landscape?

Follow the brief first. If you have a choice, let the drawings decide: long sections and linear sites suit landscape, tall buildings and vertical sections suit portrait. For several boards pinned side by side, portrait sheets often read best as one strip.

### What margin should I use on an A1 board?

Around 20 to 25 mm on every side is a sensible start. Keep it the same on every board in a set, and check your plotter's unprintable edge.

### How many drawings fit on one A1 board?

There is no fixed number. A common, readable pattern is one hero image, three to five supporting drawings, and a few small diagrams. Fewer, larger drawings usually present better than many small ones.
