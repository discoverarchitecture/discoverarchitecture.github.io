Brise-soleil in architecture

66 buildings

A brise-soleil, French for "sun breaker", is a screen that shades a facade and cuts a building's heat gain by deflecting sunlight. The simplest form is a horizontal projection on the sunny side. It is used most on glass facades that would otherwise overheat, and it still lets in the low sun of mornings, evenings and winter. Le Corbusier popularised patterned concrete versions, and Santiago Calatrava designed a moving one shaped like a wing. At the Institut du Monde Arabe, 240 photo-sensitive, motor-controlled apertures act as a brise-soleil.

Questions students ask

What is a brise-soleil?

A brise-soleil is a sunshade screen on a facade; the French name means "sun breaker", and it lowers a building's heat gain by turning sunlight away. Its plainest form is a horizontal overhang on the side facing the sun. It is used mostly on glass facades at risk of overheating, yet it still lets low morning, evening and winter sun through, which means louvres can also warm a building passively.

How did Le Corbusier use the brise-soleil?

Le Corbusier made patterned concrete versions popular. In Chandigarh, his Palace of Assembly (1962) is a parliament designed for the Punjab sun; the Secretariat Building (1958) runs a grid of brise-soleil along a long concrete slab; and the Palace of Justice (1955) shelters its courts beneath a huge concrete parasol roof. In Ahmedabad, the Mill Owners' Association Building (1954) uses angled concrete fins.

What are good examples of brise-soleil in architecture?

In Rio de Janeiro, the Gustavo Capanema Palace (1945) by Lúcio Costa and Oscar Niemeyer fits adjustable brise-soleil to an office slab, and in São Paulo Niemeyer's Copan Building (1966) runs continuous horizontal sunshades along a serpentine slab. The New York Times Building (2007) by Renzo Piano screens its glass walls with ceramic rods, and in Seoul David Chipperfield's Amorepacific Headquarters (2017) wraps a near-cube in vertical sun screens.

Can a brise-soleil move?

Yes. Santiago Calatrava designed a moving one: above a glass hall at the Milwaukee Art Museum's Quadracci Pavilion (2001), a wing-shaped sunscreen of steel fins opens and folds. In Paris, the Institut du Monde Arabe uses 240 photo-sensitive, motor-driven apertures as a brise-soleil, which open and close to control the light that reaches the interior.
