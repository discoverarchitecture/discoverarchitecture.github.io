Questions students ask

What is memorial architecture?

Memorial architecture gives memory a physical place: a memorial is any object or place on which memory or commemoration centres. Gravestones and plaques are the commonest kind, and temporary, improvised memorials are known as grassroots memorials. Mausoleums are monuments built around a burial chamber, and their name comes from Mausolus's tomb at Halicarnassus. Atatürk's mausoleum, Anitkabir in Ankara (1953), is one modern example.

Which memorials should architecture students study?

Compare the Taj Mahal (1653) in Agra, whose white marble tomb stands on a raised plinth within a quartered garden; the Gateway Arch in St. Louis (1965) by Eero Saarinen; Igualada Cemetery (1994) by Enric Miralles and Carme Pinós, a path cut into a former quarry; the Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe (2005) in Berlin by Peter Eisenman; and the National September 11 Memorial in New York (2011) by Michael Arad and Peter Walker.

Why was the Vietnam Veterans Memorial controversial?

Its colour, its lack of ornament and its form sunk below ground were what made Maya Lin's Vietnam Veterans Memorial controversial at first. The memorial, dedicated in 1982, consists of two black granite walls. It is a useful case for students because each of those three choices, material, decoration and section, is what gives the memorial its meaning, and each was what critics first objected to.

How does the Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe work?

It works through 2,711 concrete stelae of different heights, arranged on a sloping grid so that visitors walk through the narrow aisles between them. Peter Eisenman's memorial in Berlin opened in 2005. The stelae carry no names or information, which critics have said weakens its message. Compare it with the National September 11 Memorial, where two square voids mark the towers' footprints.
