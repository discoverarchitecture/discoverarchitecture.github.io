A jury deck of 10 to 15 slides is long enough to walk through a project properly and short enough that every slide has to earn its place. The structure that works is the same as a good board: idea first, then site, then the building told as a walk-through, then what is unresolved. One point per slide, drawings as large as the screen allows, and a title on each slide that says something.

## Why 10 to 15 slides

Most studio and thesis presentations run somewhere between eight and fifteen minutes before questions. At roughly a minute per slide, with some slides held longer than others, 10 to 15 slides fits that time without rushing. More slides than that usually means one of two things: slides that repeat each other, or a talk that will overrun.

If your studio gives you a different time, scale the outline. The order stays the same; you merge or split slides.

## A 15-slide outline

1. **Title and idea.** Project name, your name, and the idea in one sentence over your hero image. This slide stays up while you say your opening lines.
2. **The question.** What the brief asked, and what you decided it was really about. For a thesis, this is your research question.
3. **Site and context.** One strong map or aerial, with only the things that matter marked on it.
4. **What the site told you.** Two or three findings from your analysis, as simple diagrams. Not your whole analysis; the parts that shaped the design.
5. **Precedents.** One or two projects, and the specific thing you learned from each. "I took the stepped section from this" is useful. A page of logos and photos is not.
6. **Concept.** The diagram that explains the move. If you have a sequence of form diagrams, keep it to three or four steps.
7. **Hero view.** The image that shows the idea best, full-bleed or nearly so.
8. **Site plan.** The building in its setting: approach, entrances, ground, landscape.
9. **Plans.** The key floors, one per slide if they need it, with a scale bar and north point.
10. **Sections.** The section that explains the building, large enough to read.
11. **The key space.** The room, threshold or moment you care most about, in a perspective or a large-scale section.
12. **Structure, material and detail.** How it stands up and what it is made of, with one detail that shows care.
13. **Climate and performance.** Light, air, shade, water or energy, where they shaped the design. Skip this if they didn't; don't add it for show.
14. **Model.** Photographs of the physical model, if you made one, taken against a plain background in good light.
15. **What's next.** What is unresolved, and how you would take it forward. Then go back to the hero image and stop talking.

For a 10-slide deck, merge site and site findings, fold precedents into the concept slide, combine structure and climate, and put model photos alongside the key space.

## Rules for every slide

- **One point per slide.** If you need to say "and also" to explain a slide, it is two slides.
- **Titles that say something.** "Sections" is a label. "The roof steps down to the courtyard to bring winter sun in" is a point. The jury reads the title before they look at the drawing.
- **Drawings large.** Crop tight, use the whole slide, and keep text off the drawing where you can. A plan the size of a postage stamp in the middle of a white slide wastes the screen.
- **Few words.** A title and a caption at most. If you need a paragraph, you need to say it rather than show it.
- **Consistent frame.** The same title position, margins, type and background on every slide. Choose white or dark and stay with it.
- **Scale and orientation.** Scale bars and north points on every plan, as on the board. A projected drawing has no fixed scale, so a ratio alone means nothing on screen.

## What's different about a thesis presentation

A thesis deck usually needs more at the front: the research question, why it matters, and how you went about answering it. That can take two or three slides instead of one. Keep the research section short enough that the design still gets more than half of the time. Jurors at a thesis review often say the research was interesting but they wanted to see the building; don't give them the chance.

Be explicit about the link between research and design. A slide that says "finding → design decision" in two columns, with one or two rows, does more than five slides of findings followed by five of drawings.

## The deck should follow the board

If you also pin up a board, the deck should tell the same story in the same order, with the same drawings and the same words. The board is the summary the jury can scan; the deck is the walk-through you narrate. Nobody, including you, should have to learn two versions of the project.

This is also why it is easier to make the board first. Once the board's reading order is settled, the deck is mostly a matter of giving each part of the board its own slide. ## Rehearse with the deck, not just the slides

Click through the deck out loud, standing up, with a timer, at least twice. Mark the slides where you stumble; those usually need a better title or a cleaner drawing rather than more practice. Check the deck on the projector or screen you will use if you possibly can. Thin lines and pale greys that look fine on a laptop can vanish on a projector.

## Export, backup, and the morning of

- **Export a PDF** as well as the original file. Fonts and links break; a PDF opens almost anywhere.
- **Keep two copies**, one on a USB stick and one in cloud storage you can reach from someone else's laptop.
- **Bring your own adapter** for the projector or screen, and your charger.
- **Put the deck on presenter view** if it shows you the next slide, so you know what's coming without turning around.

## FAQ

### How many slides should an architecture jury presentation have?

For a talk of roughly eight to fifteen minutes, 10 to 15 slides is a good range. Fewer, clearer slides are better than more slides shown quickly.

### What should be on the first slide?

The project name, your name, and the idea of the project in one sentence over your strongest image. That slide stays up while you open your talk.

### Should my jury deck match my presentation board?

Yes. Use the same order, the same drawings and the same words, so the deck reads as a walk-through of the board rather than a second version of the project.
