Pavilion architecture

58 buildings

A pavilion is a smaller building set apart from, or attached to, a main building, and it was traditionally built for pleasure and relaxation. The word comes from a large tent raised on posts. World's fairs, art biennales and national fairs use national pavilions, and most are temporary, taken down when the fair closes. The Giardini in Venice holds 30 permanent national pavilions, some by leading 20th-century architects. Since 2000 the Serpentine has commissioned a summer pavilion from a leading architect every year.

Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich's Barcelona Pavilion (1929) sets free-standing walls under a floating roof, so space flows instead of forming rooms. In Hanover, the Japan Pavilion, Expo 2000 by Shigeru Ban with Frei Otto was a gridshell of recycled paper tubes, designed to be recycled afterwards, while MVRDV's Netherlands Pavilion, Expo 2000 stacked Dutch landscapes, from dunes to a forest floor, into a vertical park. He Jingtang's China Pavilion, Expo 2010 in Shanghai cantilevers an inverted pyramid of red beams modelled on dougong timber brackets. John Nash's Royal Pavilion in Brighton (1823) shows the older meaning: a building for pleasure, crowned with bulbous domes.

Questions students ask

What is a pavilion in architecture?

A pavilion is a secondary building, either standing apart from a main building or joined to it, and traditionally it was made for pleasure and rest. The word comes from a big tent held up on posts. Today it also means the national pavilions at world's fairs and art biennales, which are mostly temporary and dismantled once the fair ends.

Why are pavilions important in architecture?

Pavilions matter because, being small and often temporary, they allow architects to try out an idea at full scale. In the Barcelona Pavilion (1929) by Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich, freestanding walls stand beneath a floating roof, and space flows rather than forming rooms. At Expo 2000 in Hanover, Shigeru Ban's Japan Pavilion was a gridshell made of recycled paper tubes, and Buckminster Fuller's Montreal Biosphère (1967) is a geodesic dome of steel tubes.

What is the Serpentine Pavilion?

The Serpentine Pavilion is a temporary summer pavilion that the Serpentine Galleries in London have commissioned each year since 2000, always from a leading architect. Like most fair pavilions, each one comes down when its season ends, so it serves as a full-scale test of an architect's ideas. Venice's Giardini offers a permanent counterpart, with 30 national pavilions, some designed by major 20th-century architects, for example Sverre Fehn's Nordic Pavilion (1962).

Which world's fair pavilions should architecture students study?

Compare the Barcelona Pavilion (1929); the Atomium in Brussels (1958), nine steel spheres in an iron-crystal pattern; the Montreal Biosphère (1967); Álvaro Siza's Portugal Pavilion in Lisbon (1998), whose thin concrete canopy hangs like a sheet between two porticoes; the Netherlands Pavilion by MVRDV (Expo 2000), a vertical park built from stacked Dutch landscapes; and the China Pavilion by He Jingtang (Expo 2010), based on dougong timber brackets.
