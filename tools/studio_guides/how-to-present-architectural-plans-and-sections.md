A plan or section reads clearly when the eye can tell, instantly, what is cut, what is seen beyond, and what is far away. That comes down to three things: a line-weight hierarchy that starts at the cut, poché that is consistent across every drawing, and restraint with colour. None of it needs special software, only a few decisions made once and applied everywhere.

## Start from the cut

Every plan and section is a cut through the building. The things the cutting plane passes through, such as walls, slabs, columns and the ground, are the most important lines in the drawing, so they get the heaviest weight. Things seen beyond the cut, like a wall in elevation, a stair going up, or a window sill below, are lighter. Things far away, hidden or overhead are lightest, and often dashed.

When this hierarchy is clear, a juror can read depth in a flat drawing at a glance. When every line is the same weight, the drawing goes flat and grey, and the jury has to work out what is wall and what is furniture.

## A line-weight set you can use

Pen sizes in architectural drawing traditionally follow a standard series: 0.13, 0.18, 0.25, 0.35, 0.5, 0.7 and 1.0 mm. Three or four weights are enough for most drawings. As a starting point for a plan or section printed at 1:100:

- **Cut lines (walls, slabs, ground in section):** 0.5 to 0.7 mm.
- **Edges seen beyond the cut (steps, sills, walls in elevation):** 0.25 to 0.35 mm.
- **Distant elements, furniture, surface patterns, hatching:** 0.13 to 0.18 mm.
- **Hidden or overhead elements (roof edge, beams above):** the thinnest weight, dashed.

At 1:200 or 1:500 the same drawing gets smaller, so drop each weight a step or simplify the drawing. At 1:50 you can go heavier and show more layers. Whatever you choose, use the same set on every drawing in the set. Mixed line weights between drawings, from different stages or software, are one of the main reasons a board looks unfinished.

Check line weights in print, not on screen. A screen zooms; paper doesn't. Print a section of the drawing at full size, or at least at A3, before committing to the plot.

## Poché: solid, grey or none

Poché is the fill inside cut elements. You have three common options:

- **Solid black.** Strong and clear, especially at small scales (1:200 and smaller) where thin walls would otherwise disappear. It can make a plan heavy if walls are thick.
- **Grey or a mid-tone.** Softer, and it lets the heavy cut line still read around the fill. Often a good choice at 1:100.
- **No fill, heavy outline only.** Clean and light, but depends on strong line weights to read.

Choose one approach and use it on every plan and section. If one plan is solid black and the next is grey, the jury reads them as different kinds of drawing.

In sections, decide how to show the ground. A solid or hatched band below the ground line makes the cut through the earth read, and makes the relationship between the building and the ground obvious. Keep it the same tone as the poché, or one step lighter.

## One accent colour, one meaning

Most plans and sections read best in black, white and greys. If you add colour, add one, and give it one meaning: the public route through the building, the new intervention in an existing structure, the shared space your project is about, the path of light or air. Use it on every drawing for that meaning only.

The accent then becomes a thread the jury follows across the board. Pick a colour that still reads when printed (some bright screen colours print dull) and that works for colour-blind readers; check it in greyscale to make sure it still separates from the black and greys. Avoid colour-coding every room by function unless the brief asks for it; it turns a plan into a diagram.

## Labels, scale bars and north points

- **Scale bar on every plan and section.** A ratio like "1:100" is only true at one print size. Boards get printed at A3 for checks, shown on screens, and put in portfolios; a scale bar stays true at any size.
- **North point on every plan,** same style and size each time, ideally with plans all oriented the same way.
- **Room labels** in one small, plain typeface, the same size throughout. On busy plans, number the rooms and put a key beside the drawing.
- **Cut lines on plans** that show where each section is taken, labelled to match the section titles.
- **Levels** on sections, marked consistently, so the jury can read heights without asking.

## People, trees and furniture

Entourage gives scale and life, but it is the easiest way to clutter a drawing. Use line-only figures and trees in the lightest weight, so they sit behind the architecture. Put people where they explain how a space is used, not in every room. Furniture should show what a room is for; a few key pieces are usually enough.

## Make every drawing match

The test of a set of drawings is that they look like they came from one hand. Before you lay out the board, put all your plans and sections side by side and check:

1. Same line-weight set, mapped the same way.
2. Same poché approach and tone.
3. Same label typeface, size and position.
4. Same scale bar and north point style.
5. Same accent colour, used for the same meaning.

## FAQ

### What line weights should I use for architectural plans?

A common starting point at 1:100 is 0.5 to 0.7 mm for cut walls, 0.25 to 0.35 mm for edges seen beyond the cut, and 0.13 to 0.18 mm for furniture, hatching and distant elements. Use the same set across every drawing and check it in print.

### What is poché in architecture?

Poché is the fill inside the elements a plan or section cuts through, such as walls, slabs and the ground. It can be solid black, grey or left empty with a heavy outline; what matters is using one approach consistently.

### Should architectural plans be in colour?

Usually black, white and grey read best. If you use colour, use one accent with one meaning, applied across every drawing, and check that it still reads when printed.
