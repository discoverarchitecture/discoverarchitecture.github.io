School and campus architecture

229 buildings

School and campus architecture matters because the design of a building can strongly shape how students learn. A campus traditionally means the land and buildings of a college or university. In 1922 Patrick Abercrombie contrasted the American campus, its departments scattered about a park among the trees, with the formal quadrangles of Oxford and Cambridge. Gothic, which flourished in English universities from the 13th to the 15th centuries, was later revived for campuses. Brutalism was used on university campuses around the world in the 1960s and 1970s, and in the same years open classrooms became popular in North America. Walter Gropius founded the Bauhaus in Weimar on 1 April 1919.

Gropius's Bauhaus Dessau (1926) wraps the school's workshops in a glass curtain wall on a pinwheel plan. William Pereira's Geisel Library in San Diego (1970) is a campus building of that brutalist era: its reading floors stack outward like an inverted stepped pyramid on splayed concrete piers above a glazed base. Foster + Partners' Apple Park in Cupertino (2017) takes the campus idea into the workplace: a ring of curved glass around a planted courtyard.
