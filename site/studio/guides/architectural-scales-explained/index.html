Architectural scales explained: 1:500 to 1:5, and 1/8" = 1'-0"

An architectural scale tells you how much smaller the drawing is than the real thing: at 1:100, one centimetre on paper is one metre in the building. Small scales such as 1:500 suit sites, 1:100 and 1:50 suit whole buildings, and 1:20 to 1:5 suit rooms and details. In the United States the same jobs are done by inch scales, such as 1/8" = 1'-0" (1:96) and 1/4" = 1'-0" (1:48). The number is only true at one print size, so every drawing also needs a scale bar.

What a scale ratio means

1:100 means one unit on the drawing stands for 100 of the same units in reality, so one centimetre on paper is one metre. A 10 m wall drawn at 1:100 is 100 mm long on the sheet; at 1:50 it is 200 mm, and at 1:200 it is 50 mm.

The bigger the second number, the smaller the drawing and the less detail it holds. So 1:20 is a "larger" scale than 1:500, which confuses many first-year students.

US scales give a length on paper for one foot of building. At 1/4" = 1'-0", a quarter inch is one foot; a foot is 12 inches, and 12 divided by 1/4 is 48, so the ratio is 1:48. A 20 ft wall at quarter-inch scale is 5 inches long.

The common scales and what each is for

These are typical uses, not rules. If your brief or school asks for something else, follow it.

  • 1:2500 to 1:1000: the wider area, location plans, urban studies.
  • 1:500: the site and its neighbours, masterplans, large site plans.
  • 1:200: site plans of small sites, and plans of large buildings.
  • 1:100: plans, sections and elevations of most buildings; the usual default for student projects.
  • 1:50: houses, small buildings and interiors, with furniture and wall layers.
  • 1:20: room layouts, stairs, kitchens, wall sections.
  • 1:10 and 1:5: construction details, junctions, joinery.
  • 1:1: full size, for a profile, a joint or a material sample.

Anything drawn without a true scale, such as a concept diagram, is marked NTS, "not to scale".

Metric and US imperial side by side

The exact ratios, with the nearest metric scale:

  • 1/16" = 1'-0" is 1:192 (near 1:200).
  • 1/8" = 1'-0" is 1:96 (near 1:100).
  • 1/4" = 1'-0" is 1:48 (near 1:50).
  • 1/2" = 1'-0" is 1:24 (near 1:20 or 1:25).
  • 3/4" = 1'-0" is 1:16.
  • 1" = 1'-0" is 1:12 (near 1:10).
  • 3" = 1'-0" is 1:4 (near 1:5).

US site plans often use engineering scales instead: 1" = 20' is 1:240 and 1" = 40' is 1:480 (near 1:500).

"Near" matters. A 30 m building drawn at 1:96 is 312.5 mm on paper, so a 1:100 ruler reads it as 31.25 m, about 4% too long. Check the stated scale of any precedent or base plan before you measure it.

How to read a scale ruler

Metric scale ruler

A triangular metric scale ruler has six edges, one scale each. Sets vary: a common one is 1:20, 1:25, 1:50, 1:75, 1:100 and 1:125; another runs from 1:100 to 1:500. The numbers are real metres, so if the 1:100 edge reads "5", the wall is 5 m long.

Each edge also works at ten times or a tenth of its scale: the 1:100 edge reads 1:1000 if you multiply by ten, and 1:10 if you divide by ten.

US architect's scale

The triangular architect's scale usually carries eleven scales: full size in sixteenths of an inch, plus five pairs (3/32 and 3/16, 1/8 and 1/4, 1/2 and 1, 3/8 and 3/4, 1 1/2 and 3). Each pair shares an edge, read from opposite ends, so check the label at the end you are reading from.

Beyond each zero, one foot is divided into inches (or groups of inches on the smallest scales). Put a whole-foot mark on one end of the line so the other end falls in that divided foot, and add the inches to the feet.

US engineer's scale

This one is divided into 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 parts per inch, for site plans. On the 20 edge, each division is one foot at 1" = 20', or ten feet at 1" = 200'.

Which scale for which drawing on a jury board

Choose a scale by measuring the space, not by habit: divide the real length by the space on the sheet, then round up to the next common scale. For example, a 120 m site in 250 mm of board: 120,000 mm divided by 250 mm is 480, so use 1:500, and the site is 240 mm long.

Another example: a building 60 m by 25 m is 600 × 250 mm at 1:100, wider than a portrait A1 sheet (594 mm). Draw it at 1:200 (300 × 125 mm) or turn the board. Sheet dimensions are in our sheet sizes guide.

  • Plans, sections and elevations at one scale, so the jury can compare them and a section lines up with its plan.
  • The site plan two to five times smaller, such as 1:500 with 1:100 plans.
  • One enlarged part, such as a 1:20 wall section, if the brief asks for detail.
  • Scaled drawings placed first, because their size is fixed; the rest fits around them, as in laying out an A1 board.

The scale also decides how much to draw: at 1:200 a wall is one heavy line or a thin band; at 1:50 it shows its layers. Line weights change with it, as presenting plans and sections explains.

Scale bars, and why they matter

A ratio such as 1:100 is true at one print size only. The same board is printed at A3 for checks, projected, and put in portfolios, and at each of those sizes the written ratio is wrong. A scale bar grows and shrinks with the drawing, so it stays right.

  • Put one under every plan, section, elevation and site plan.
  • Use round numbers in real units: 0, 1, 5, 10 m for a building, 0, 50, 100, 200 m for a site, 0, 4, 8, 16 ft in imperial.
  • If you also write the ratio, add the sheet size: "1:100 at A1".

Printing or exporting at the wrong size

The usual culprit is the print dialogue: "fit to page" shrinks the sheet, often by a few per cent, and the drawing is no longer at 1:100. Choose "actual size" or 100%.

A smaller sheet changes every scale. An A1 board printed on A3 is at 50%, so 1:100 becomes 1:200; on A2 it is about 71%, so 1:100 becomes roughly 1:141, which matches no ruler. A 24 × 36 inch sheet printed on 12 × 18 is exactly half size, so 1/4" = 1'-0" becomes 1/8" = 1'-0"; on 11 × 17 it is a little under half, and neither scale holds.

In CAD or BIM software, set the scale in the plot or sheet layout, on the real paper size, and export a PDF at that size. Then check:

  1. Open the PDF and confirm the page size is the sheet size, not A4 or Letter.
  2. Measure the printed scale bar with a ruler: at 1:100, the 10 m mark should be 100 mm.
  3. No scale bar? Measure a known dimension, such as a door.

Model scales

Models use the same ratios. Typical choices: 1:1000 or 1:500 for site and massing models (US: 1" = 40', which is 1:480), 1:200 or 1:100 for a whole building (1/16" or 1/8"), 1:50 for a house or interior (1/4"), and 1:20 or 1:10 for a detail (1/2" or 1").

Figures need the same scale. A 1.75 m person is 17.5 mm tall at 1:100, 35 mm at 1:50 and 8.75 mm at 1:200. Ready-made figures often come in model railway scales such as HO (1:87), slightly too big for 1:100. Camera height follows the scale too; photographing models gives eye heights.

Questions students ask

What scale should a floor plan be?

For most student projects, 1:100, or 1/8" = 1'-0" in the US. Use 1:50 or 1/4" = 1'-0" for houses and interiors, and 1:200 or 1/16" = 1'-0" for large buildings, unless your brief says otherwise.

What does 1:100 scale mean?

It means one unit on the drawing equals 100 of the same units in reality, so 1 cm on paper is 1 m in the building. A 10 m wall at 1:100 is 100 mm long on the sheet.

How do I print a drawing to scale?

Set the scale in your software's plot or sheet layout, export a PDF at the real sheet size, and print at 100% or "actual size", never "fit to page". Then measure the printed scale bar with a ruler.
