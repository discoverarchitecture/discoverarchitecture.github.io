About Roark.

Howard Roark is the architect of The Fountainhead, the novel Ayn Rand published in 1943. Its first sentence is three words long: “Howard Roark laughed.” He has just been expelled from architecture school for refusing to draw in borrowed styles, and he does not mind.

The book follows him through a career of lost commissions. He works for Henry Cameron, an old master the city has stopped hiring. He breaks stone in a quarry rather than add a classical portico to a design. His classmate Peter Keating does the opposite: he copies, flatters, takes credit for other men's drawings and rises fast. When a housing project Roark designed is built against his plans, he dynamites it, and at the trial he explains why a building belongs to the mind that conceived it. The jury acquits him. King Vidor filmed the story in 1949, with Gary Cooper as Roark.

Readers have seen Frank Lloyd Wright in him since the book appeared. The likeness that matters here is plainer. Roark builds what the site, the programme and the material ask for, and signs it.

The archive takes his name for that reason. It holds 6,987 buildings by 388 architects, each with the people who made it, the year, the place, and the drawings that show how it works: plans, sections, elevations. A photograph shows what a building looks like. A plan shows what it decided. Roark keeps both, credits both, and leaves out the pastiche.
