Housing architecture

264 buildings

Housing architecture designs homes in numbers, and much of its history is the history of public or social housing: subsidised or affordable homes provided in larger buildings. Modern municipal housing began as a response to fast urban population growth. In Britain, mass council house building began around 1920, after the Addison Act of 1919, and tower blocks followed in the 1950s. Red Vienna placed large blocks around green courts. Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation turned housing into a modernist type, with "streets in the sky" and a shared roof terrace.

Questions students ask

What is social housing in architecture?

Social housing, also called public housing, means subsidised or affordable homes, usually in larger buildings. Municipal housing in its modern form grew out of rapid population growth in cities. Britain started building council houses on a mass scale around 1920, following the 1919 Addison Act, with tower blocks arriving in the 1950s. Red Vienna set large blocks such as the Karl-Marx-Hof (1930) around green courts.

Which housing projects should architecture students study?

In date order: the Hufeisensiedlung (1927) in Berlin by Bruno Taut and Martin Wagner, a horseshoe wrapped around a pond; the Weissenhof Estate in Stuttgart (1927); Vienna's Karl-Marx-Hof (1930); the Unité d'Habitation (1952) by Le Corbusier in Marseille; Park Hill in Sheffield (1961); Habitat 67 in Montréal (1967); the Barbican Estate in London (1982); and Quinta Monroy (2004) in Iquique, Chile, by Alejandro Aravena.

What does "streets in the sky" mean?

"Streets in the sky" means housing in which broad access corridors or decks act as streets raised above the ground. The Unité d'Habitation (1952) in Marseille, by Le Corbusier, holds 337 flats, with an internal street on every third floor and a communal terrace on its flat roof. Park Hill (1961) in Sheffield moves those streets outdoors as broad concrete decks following the slope, and London's Balfron Tower (1967) connects its flats to a separate service tower by bridges.

What is incremental housing?

Incremental housing builds part of a home properly and leaves residents to complete the rest when they can. At Quinta Monroy (2004) in Iquique, Chile, Alejandro Aravena built half of a good house for each family and left them the other half to finish; the approach helped him win the 2016 Pritzker Prize. It is a useful precedent for any student project on affordable housing.
