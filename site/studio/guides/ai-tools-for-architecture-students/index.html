AI tools for architecture students: an honest roundup

AI tools can save an architecture student real time, but mostly on the work around design: reading, writing, rendering atmosphere, laying out and checking. None of them designs a good building for you, and a jury will find that out within a minute of questions. This roundup groups the tools by what they are for, describes each one plainly, and says where it helps and where it gets in the way. It includes our own tool, and says so.

Two notes before the list. Products in this space change quickly, so check each tool's current features, pricing and student terms on its own site. And check your school's policy on AI before you use any of them for assessed work; the rules vary a lot between schools and even between studios.

General chat assistants

Examples: ChatGPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google).

Useful for: unpacking a long brief into a list of requirements, drafting and tightening your project statement, turning notes into captions, summarising a reading so you know which parts to read closely, and rehearsing for a jury by asking it to play a critical juror and question your scheme.

Watch out for: confident mistakes. These assistants can state wrong facts about buildings, architects and regulations, and can invent references that don't exist. Never cite something only because an assistant said it; find the source. Don't paste in material you aren't allowed to share, such as a client's documents in an internship.

Image generators

Examples: Midjourney, Adobe Firefly, and Stable Diffusion (open models you can run yourself or through various services).

Useful for: mood and atmosphere images early on, exploring material and light ideas, quick backgrounds and skies, and visual references when you can't find a photograph of what you have in mind.

Watch out for: drift. A text-to-image generator doesn't know your plan or section, so it will happily produce a building that can't be what you drew. Presenting such an image as a view of your design is misleading, and jurors who compare it with your plans will notice. Stable Diffusion with extensions such as ControlNet can follow a line drawing or depth map much more closely, at the cost of more setup and a capable computer. Ownership and copyright rules around generated images are still unsettled in many places, so credit them as AI-generated.

Rendering from your own model

Examples: Veras by EvolveLAB (an add-in for modelling software such as Revit, SketchUp and Rhino), and AI features now appearing inside established rendering and image-editing software, such as generative fill in Adobe Photoshop.

Useful for: quick atmospheric variations of a view taken from your own 3D model, so the geometry starts from your design. Generative fill is handy for adding or extending people, planting and sky in a render or photo.

Watch out for: small changes to your geometry, such as extra windows, different roof lines or merged columns, especially at higher "creativity" settings. Compare every output with your model before it goes on a board. Treat these as a sketching aid for mood, and keep a conventional render or a careful hand-finished view for the image that must be accurate.

Early-stage design and site analysis

Examples: Autodesk Forma (cloud-based early-stage planning and environmental analysis, such as sun, daylight, wind and noise), and TestFit (site feasibility, aimed mainly at real-estate development).

Useful for: testing massing options against sun and wind on a real site, and understanding how practice uses fast feasibility studies. Being able to say "I tested three massing options for winter sun" with evidence strengthens a jury presentation.

Watch out for: treating outputs as answers. Analysis tools show consequences of options you give them; the design decision is still yours to explain. Check whether your school provides access, since professional software licences can be expensive.

Floor-plan generators

Examples: Maket (generates residential floor-plan options from a brief and constraints) and Finch (generates and tests floor-plan layouts).

Useful for: seeing many layout options quickly, checking areas, and understanding how constraint-based design tools work. They are more relevant to housing and repetitive programmes than to a one-off studio building.

Watch out for: presenting a generated plan as your design. A jury will ask why the stair is where it is, and "the tool put it there" isn't an answer. If you use one, use it to explore, then design and justify your own plan.

Presentation and layout tools

General slide makers, such as Gamma, Beautiful.ai and Canva's AI features, turn text into tidy decks quickly. How to judge an AI architecture presentation generator

Tools that promise to turn your work into a finished presentation are new, and they differ more than their landing pages suggest. Whichever one you try, including ours, ask these questions before you trust it with a jury:

  • Does it start from your drawings? A generator that invents images from a text prompt gives you a board about a building you didn't design. One that places, cleans up and lays out your own plans, sections and sketches keeps the work yours.
  • Can you see the result before paying? A full preview of the board and the deck tells you more than any sample gallery.
  • Is the output editable? You will need to move a drawing, fix a label or swap an image the night before. Look for files you can open in the programs you already use, such as PowerPoint or InDesign, not only a flat image.
  • Does it know architectural sheets? Real sizes (A1, A0, 24 × 36), margins, a grid, and drawings at a sensible scale with scale bars, rather than a slide template stretched to a poster.
  • Is it honest about what it made up? Any drawing or image the tool generates should be marked as generated, and estimated dimensions should be clearly estimates.
  • What happens to your files? Check how long uploads are kept and whether they are used to train models. Your design work is yours.
  • How is it priced? A one-off payment per board, a subscription, or credits that run out: know what a single jury will cost you.

Using any of them without getting into trouble

  • Know your school's rules and follow them, including any requirement to declare AI use.
  • Credit AI-generated images on your board, for example with a small "AI-assisted image" note.
  • Never let AI supply facts about your site, areas, dates, precedents or regulations without checking them yourself.
  • Keep your originals and your process work. If anyone questions your authorship, your sketches and models are the evidence.
  • Check everything before you print. You present the board, so every line on it is your responsibility.
  • Spend the saved time on design. The best use of these tools is buying back hours for the parts only you can do: thinking, drawing and testing the idea. For drawing quality itself, see presenting plans and sections.

Questions students ask

Is it allowed to use AI in architecture school?

It depends on your school and often on your studio tutor. Some allow it with a declaration, some restrict it for assessed work. Check the written policy and ask your tutor if it's unclear.

Can AI design my floor plans?

Some tools generate plan options, but they don't replace design. A jury will ask you to justify every decision, so use generated options to explore, then draw and defend your own plan.

Will my jury know if I used AI?

Often, yes, especially if an image doesn't match your drawings. Be open about it, credit AI-generated images, and make sure everything you present is consistent and something you can explain.

Is there an AI that makes architecture presentation boards?

Yes, a few tools now do parts of it: general slide makers lay out text and images, and architecture-specific generators such as Studio FGA (ours) build an A1 board and jury deck from your own drawings. Check that any such tool starts from your drawings, shows a preview, and gives you files you can edit.
