Good model photographs need very little equipment: a phone, a plain backdrop, one light coming low from the side, and something to hold the phone still. The difference between a snapshot and a board-ready photograph is mostly about where the light comes from, where the camera sits, and keeping every photograph in the series the same. Here is a method that works in a studio corner the night before a jury.

## What you need

- **A phone.** Any recent phone camera is good enough for an A1 board. Turn on the camera's grid lines; they help keep verticals straight.
- **Something to hold it still:** a small tripod with a phone clamp, or a stack of books. A steady camera lets you use less light without blur.
- **A backdrop:** a large sheet of white, light grey or black card, curved up behind the model so there is no visible line where the table meets the wall.
- **One light:** a window with daylight, or a single desk lamp. One light gives clear shadows; several lights from different sides flatten the model.
- **White card** to bounce a little light back into the shadows, and black card if you want them deeper.
- **Scale figures** at the right scale for your model, if it doesn't have them already.

## Prepare the model

Clean it before you shoot. Dust, glue strings, pencil marks and loose fibres all show up larger in a photograph than in the room. A soft brush and a bit of tape do most of the work. Check that walls are straight, roofs sit flat and nothing is peeling; the camera exaggerates small faults.

If the base is scruffy, put the model on a clean board or the backdrop itself. Remove anything on the table that isn't part of the project.

## Choose the backdrop

- **White or light grey** is the safe choice for most boards: it sits quietly on a white or pale sheet and keeps the model the centre of attention. Grey is easier to expose than pure white.
- **Black** gives drama and works well for white models, night views and lit interiors, but it can look heavy on a pale board and hides dark materials.

Pick one and use it for every photograph of the project, so they read as a set on the board.

## Light: one source, low and from the side

Light coming from directly above or from the camera makes a model look flat. Light from the side, fairly low, throws shadows across the surfaces and shows depth, openings and texture. Place your lamp or turn the model so the light rakes across the main facade at an angle, roughly like late-afternoon sun.

- Move the light, not just the camera. Try it from the left, the right and behind; a light slightly behind the model gives a bright rim and long shadows towards the camera.
- Use the white card on the shadow side if the shadows go completely black and you lose detail.
- Avoid the phone's flash. It flattens everything and puts hard reflections on any glossy surface.
- For a sun study, point the light at the angle the sun really comes from at your site. Then the photograph is evidence, not only atmosphere.

## Put the camera where a person would stand

Eye-level views often say the most to a jury, because they show the building as someone would experience it. Work out a person's eye height at your model's scale: about 1.6 m becomes roughly 32 mm at 1:50, 16 mm at 1:100 and 8 mm at 1:200. Bring the phone's lens down to that height, at the edge of the model, and look along the street or into the courtyard.

A phone is thin enough to get surprisingly low. If the lens can't get close enough because of the phone's body, raise the model on a box at the edge of the table so the camera can sit level with its base.

## A standard set of views

Shoot the same set for every model, so you always have what the board needs:

1. **The overall view:** a three-quarter view from above, showing the whole model and its context.
2. **Elevation:** straight on to the main facade, camera level, verticals vertical.
3. **Eye level:** one or two views from a person's height, at the entrance and in the main space.
4. **Details:** a close look at a junction, a roof edge, a material.
5. **Process:** quick record shots of each earlier model, all from the same angle, if you want a row of massing studies.

## Get the photograph right in the camera

- **Keep verticals straight.** Hold the phone level, not tilted down; use the grid lines. Converging verticals make a model look like a toy.
- **Focus on the part that matters.** Tap the screen on the entrance, not the background. Close up, only part of the model will be sharp; for a fully sharp view, step back slightly and crop later.
- **Watch portrait mode.** Its artificial blur often cuts edges of the model wrongly. Use the normal photo mode for anything you will put on a board.
- **Lock the exposure.** A white backdrop fools the phone into making the picture too dark. On most phones you can press and hold on the model to lock focus and exposure, then slide brightness up a little.
- **Shoot more than you need.** Small changes of light and angle make big differences; choose later on a computer screen, not on the phone.

## Edit honestly

Light editing is normal and expected: crop, straighten verticals, balance the white, lift the shadows a little, clean up specks on the backdrop. Free apps such as Snapseed have a perspective tool for straightening.

Changing the model itself is different. Adding people, trees, a sky or a context that wasn't there, removing a mistake, or having an AI tool regenerate the model in new light makes the photograph partly a rendering. That can be fine, but say so on the board ("model photo, AI-assisted lighting"), and check the result still matches your model and drawings exactly. Jurors who handle your model and then see a different one in the photograph lose trust in the whole board. ## Putting model photographs on the board

- Use the same backdrop, colour balance and crop proportions for every model photograph in the set.
- Place each one next to the drawing it explains: the eye-level view beside the plan with the viewpoint marked, the overall view near the site plan.
- A row of process models from the same angle makes a clear [massing-moves diagram](guide:architecture-concept-diagram-ideas).
- Check resolution: a photograph that will fill a third of an A1 board should be at least about 2,500 pixels wide. [Sheet sizes and pixels](guide:architecture-sheet-sizes-a1-a0-24x36) explains the arithmetic.

## FAQ

### What is the best background for photographing architecture models?

A plain sheet of white or light grey card, curved up behind the model so there is no horizon line. It suits most boards and keeps the attention on the model. Black works for white models and dramatic or night views, but can look heavy on a pale board.

### How do I light an architecture model for photographs?

Use one light, from the side and fairly low, like late-afternoon sun: a window or a single desk lamp. It throws shadows that show depth and openings. Bounce some light into the shadows with white card if they are too dark, and avoid the phone's flash.

### Can I edit model photos with AI?

Light corrections such as cropping, straightening and colour balance are normal. If AI changes the model, the lighting or the scene, mark the photograph as AI-assisted on your board and check it still matches your model and drawings exactly. Follow your school's rules on AI use.
