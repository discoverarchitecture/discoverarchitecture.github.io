High-tech architecture

95 buildings

High-tech architecture shows how a building is made and how it works: structure, lifts, stairs and ducts are put on display instead of hidden. It emerged in the 1970s as a form of late modernism and was first developed in Britain. Steel, aluminium and glass dominate. Interiors have no internal load-bearing walls, so the space can be rearranged, and services often move outside into separate towers. The style is used most for factories, offices and galleries, and its leading architects include Hopkins, Foster, Rogers, Piano and Calatrava.

Read moreShow less

Renzo Piano and Richard Rogers's Centre Pompidou in Paris (1977) moves structure and colour-coded services outside, leaving free floors and a public piazza. Michael and Patty Hopkins's Hopkins House in London (1976) is a two-storey steel and glass box entered by a bridge. Rogers's Lloyd's Building in London (1986) sets lifts, stairs and ducts in plug-in towers around a 60-metre atrium. Norman Foster's HSBC Main Building in Hong Kong (1986) hangs its floors from suspension trusses, freeing a public plaza beneath. In Malaysia, Ken Yeang's Menara Mesiniaga (1992) cools a round tower with spiralling sky gardens and sunshades on its hot facades.
