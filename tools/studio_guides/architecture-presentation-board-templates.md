A good architecture board template is not a finished-looking design you pour your drawings into. It is a set of decisions: the sheet size, the margins, a column grid, a title block, a few text styles and a colour or two. Those decisions make a board look calm and make a set of boards look like one project. Everything else, including where the drawings go, should come from your project. Below is what to look for in a template, five structures that work, and how to build your own in an afternoon.

## Why templates disappoint

Most downloadable templates are designed around the project in their preview: a big glossy render at the top, three square images below, a block of placeholder text. Your project has a long section, two awkward plans and a set of sketches, and none of it fits the boxes. You end up cropping your best drawing to fit someone else's frame.

The other problem is sameness. Popular templates turn up on many boards in the same review, and a juror who has seen the layout before stops looking at it.

So use templates for their structure, not their look. If a template's grid, margins and type are good, keep those and throw away its boxes.

## What a good template contains

- **The real sheet size and orientation,** set up in millimetres or inches, not pixels. A1, A0, 24 × 36 or whatever your school uses; see [sheet sizes compared](guide:architecture-sheet-sizes-a1-a0-24x36).
- **Margins,** the same on every side or slightly deeper at the bottom. Around 20 to 25 mm on A1 is a sensible start.
- **A column grid with gutters,** for example six columns on landscape A1 or four on portrait A1, so any drawing can be one, two, three or more columns wide. [Laying out an A1 board](guide:how-to-lay-out-an-a1-architecture-board) works the numbers through.
- **A title block** in the same place on every board: project name, a one-line description, your name, the studio and the date, and a board number if there are several.
- **Text styles:** a title, a heading, body text, captions and labels, in one typeface family, set to sizes that read on the printed sheet.
- **One accent colour** and a light grey for context, saved as swatches.
- **Guides for a reading order,** usually top left to bottom right, so the strongest drawing sits where the eye lands first.

Notice what isn't on the list: picture boxes. A template that fixes the boxes fixes your project's story before you've told it.

## Five template structures, described

These are structures rather than designs. Set any of them on your grid, then size the drawings to your project.

### 1. Portrait A1, four columns, title at the top

A title band across the full width at the top, the hero image spanning all four columns under it, then rows of drawings two or four columns wide. The most common studio board, and the easiest to read standing in front of it.

### 2. Landscape A1, six columns, a spine on the left

The left one or two columns hold the title, the project statement, the site plan and a few diagrams. The remaining four or five columns hold the large drawings. Good for projects that need a little explaining first.

### 3. The strip of two or three boards

Two or three portrait boards pinned side by side, sharing one grid, one title position and one horizon line, so they read as one long sheet. The first board usually holds the idea and the site, the middle one the plans and sections, the last one the details and views.

### 4. The competition panel

A title band, then a strict grid of equal cells, often with a fixed area for the entry number and no names (competitions are usually anonymous). Read the competition's rules first: many fix the size, orientation, the place of the code and sometimes the number of panels.

### 5. The large final board

A0 or 36 × 48 inches, usually landscape, with a band of analysis and diagrams across the top, one large section through the middle and plans and views below. Keep the margin generous; a big sheet with a cramped edge looks unfinished.

## How to build your own template

### In InDesign or Affinity Publisher

1. Create a document at your sheet size with your margins and columns (in InDesign, Layout › Margins and Columns).
2. Put the title block on a parent page (called a master page in older versions and in Affinity), so it repeats on every board.
3. Make paragraph styles for title, heading, body, caption and label; make an object style for image frames, such as no stroke and a fill-proportionally setting.
4. Add swatches for your accent colour and context grey.
5. Save it as a template file and start every board from it.

These programs are the best fit for boards: real page sizes, styles, linked images that stay sharp, and clean PDF export.

### In Illustrator

Set one artboard per board at the real size, draw guides for margins and columns (Object › Path › Split into Grid on a rectangle the size of your margin area is a quick way to make column guides), and save character and paragraph styles. Illustrator is comfortable for diagram-heavy boards; keep large photographs linked rather than embedded so files stay manageable.

### In PowerPoint, Keynote or Google Slides

Set a custom slide size equal to your sheet (PowerPoint lets you type A1's 59.4 × 84.1 cm directly), then use guides for margins and columns and a slide master for the title block. It works, and many students already know these programs, but check the exported PDF at full size before you plot: image compression settings can soften photographs, so turn compression off where the program allows it.

### In Canva or similar

Set custom dimensions in millimetres or inches, add your own grid with guides, and save your text styles as a brand kit if your plan includes one. Watch the export resolution and check that line drawings stay sharp.

## Using a template without looking like everyone else

- Keep the grid and the type; redesign the boxes around your own drawings.
- Let your hero image decide the top of the board, not a placeholder.
- Change the accent colour to one that belongs to your project: a material, the site, the concept.
- Use the same template for every board in a set, but not necessarily for every project in your portfolio.
- Check font licences on downloaded templates; a font that isn't on the plotting computer will be swapped for a default.

## FAQ

### Where can I get a free architecture presentation board template?

You can build a better one yourself in an afternoon: a document at your sheet size with margins, a column grid, a title block and a few text styles. Free and paid templates exist online, but use them for their grid and type, not their picture boxes, and check their font licences.

### What is the best software for an architecture presentation board?

InDesign or Affinity Publisher are the best fit, because they handle real sheet sizes, styles and linked images, and export clean PDFs. Illustrator suits diagram-heavy boards; PowerPoint and Canva work if you set the real size and check the exported PDF before printing.

### Should every board in a set use the same template?

Yes. The same size, margins, grid, title position and type make a set of boards read as one project. The arrangement of drawings inside the grid can change from board to board.
