High-tech architecture

96 buildings

High-tech architecture shows how a building is made and how it works: structure, lifts, stairs and ducts are put on display instead of hidden. It emerged in the 1970s as a form of late modernism and was first developed in Britain. Steel, aluminium and glass dominate. Interiors have no internal load-bearing walls, so the space can be rearranged, and services often move outside into separate towers. The style is used most for factories, offices and galleries, and its leading architects include Hopkins, Foster, Rogers, Piano and Calatrava.

Renzo Piano and Richard Rogers's Centre Pompidou in Paris (1977) moves structure and colour-coded services outside, leaving free floors and a public piazza. Michael and Patty Hopkins's Hopkins House in London (1976) is a two-storey steel and glass box entered by a bridge. Rogers's Lloyd's Building in London (1986) sets lifts, stairs and ducts in plug-in towers around a 60-metre atrium. Norman Foster's HSBC Main Building in Hong Kong (1986) hangs its floors from suspension trusses, freeing a public plaza beneath. In Malaysia, Ken Yeang's Menara Mesiniaga (1992) cools a round tower with spiralling sky gardens and sunshades on its hot facades.

Questions students ask

What is high-tech architecture?

High-tech architecture puts the making and working of a building on show: frame, lifts, stairs and ducts stay visible rather than concealed. It appeared in the 1970s as a kind of late modernism, developed first in Britain. Steel, aluminium and glass are its main materials, and it is often used for factories, offices and galleries.

What are the key features of high-tech architecture?

Its interiors avoid internal load-bearing walls, leaving floors that can be rearranged, and services like stairs and lifts are often moved out to separate towers. London's Lloyd's Building (1986) wraps a 60-metre atrium with plug-in towers holding its lifts, stairs and ducts, and Hong Kong's HSBC Main Building (1986) suspends its floors from suspension trusses, which frees a public plaza underneath.

Who are the main high-tech architects?

The leading names are Michael and Patty Hopkins, Norman Foster, Richard Rogers, Renzo Piano and Santiago Calatrava. Many early leaders were British. Their key works on the wall include the Hopkins House in London (1976), Piano and Rogers's Centre Pompidou in Paris (1977), Foster's 30 St Mary Axe in London (2004) and Calatrava's Museum of Tomorrow in Rio de Janeiro (2015).

Which high-tech buildings should architecture students study?

Start with the Centre Pompidou in Paris (1977), whose frame and colour-coded services sit on the outside, leaving open floors and a public piazza. Then set beside it the Lloyd's Building (1986), the HSBC Main Building (1986), Renzo Piano's New York Times Building (2007), screened by ceramic rods, and Menara Mesiniaga (1992) in Malaysia by Ken Yeang, a round tower cooled by sky gardens.
