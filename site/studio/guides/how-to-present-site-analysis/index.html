How to do an architecture site analysis, and present it on a board and at a jury

A site analysis is the study you make of a site before you design on it: what surrounds it, how people reach it, how sun, wind and noise act on it, how the ground moves, what was there before and what the rules allow. You gather it by visiting, reading maps, taking photographs and drawing sections. On the board it is not everything you found out. It is the three to five findings that changed your design, drawn on one consistent base map, each paired with the move it led to. A jury should be able to point at any part of your building and find the reason for it in the analysis.

What a site analysis covers

Most studio briefs expect the same topics. Look at all of them; you only know which ones matter once you have looked.

  • Context and grain. What lies within a few minutes' walk: uses, landmarks, open spaces; how big the buildings are, how close they stand, what the street wall is like.
  • Access and movement. How people arrive on foot, by bike, by public transport and by car; where service vehicles get in; which edges are busy and which are quiet.
  • Uses and activity. What happens around the site and when. A street can be empty at nine in the morning and packed at nine at night.
  • Sun and climate. Where the sun is through the day and the year; temperature, rainfall and humidity. Outside the tropics, the midday sun is to the south in the northern hemisphere and to the north in the southern hemisphere.
  • Wind. The prevailing winds from weather data, and local effects: gaps that funnel wind, gusty corners of tall blocks, sheltered pockets.
  • Views. What the site looks at, what looks back, and which views to frame or screen.
  • Ground and nature. Levels, slopes, drainage, water, soil and trees worth keeping.
  • Noise. Roads, railways, schools, markets, bars: what you hear, from which side and at what time.
  • History and character. What stood there before, how the area grew, materials and building traditions nearby, anything listed or protected.
  • Rules. Setbacks, height limits, plot coverage and heritage controls, usually given in the brief; in practice they come from the local zoning code or planning policy.

How to gather it

Visit, and go more than once

Nothing replaces standing on the site. Take a printed base map, a pen and a phone. Walk the boundary, then the routes people use to reach it. If you can, go twice, say a weekday morning and an evening. Mark the map as you go: noise, shade, where people gather, wind, views. Write the time beside each note. A note without a time is half a fact.

Maps and aerial images

Work from one clean base map. A scale of 1:500 or 1:1000 usually suits the site itself, and 1:2500 or smaller the wider area. Redraw what you need rather than pasting screenshots: OpenStreetMap is free to use with the credit "© OpenStreetMap contributors", and many national mapping agencies publish open data, while commercial map screenshots usually aren't yours to print. Aerial images help with roof shapes, trees and the grain of the area; for history, the historical imagery in Google Earth Pro and old maps in a city archive or library show how the site changed.

Photographs, numbered on a key plan

Photograph from where a person stands. Cover each edge, the views out, the neighbours and the details: paving, walls, the tree you want to keep. Number every photo and mark its position and direction on a key plan the same day; a hundred unlabelled photos are hard to use a week later.

Sections and climate data

Draw at least one section through the site and its neighbours, and if the ground slopes, one in each direction, with levels from contours or the survey in your brief. Don't draw a sun path or wind rose from memory: weather files for most cities can be downloaded free, and tools such as Ladybug for Grasshopper or Climate Consultant turn them into sun paths and wind roses for your exact location.

Analysis is an argument, not an inventory

Students often present site analysis as a catalogue: location map, land use, traffic, sun path, wind rose, photographs, a SWOT table. Each sheet is accurate, and together they say nothing, because the jury can't tell which facts mattered.

Start from the other end. List the decisions in your design that come from the site: why the entrance is on that street, why the building steps down, why the courtyard faces that way, why the tree stays. Then present the analysis that explains those decisions, and leave the rest in your sketchbook. If you can't connect a map to a decision, it doesn't go on the board.

The strongest way to show this is as pairs, a finding and then the response:

  • The main road is busy all day, the back lane quiet → the shops face the road and the quiet rooms face the lane.
  • The ground falls three metres across the site → the building steps down, with an entrance at each level.
  • The best view and the harshest afternoon sun come from the same side → deep shading that still lets you see out.
  • Two large trees stand near the middle of the site → the courtyard is planned around them.

These are made-up examples; yours come from your own site. Lay them out as a short row on the board, or a slide each in the deck. When a juror asks "why is the entrance there?", you point at the pair.

The drawings that do the work

One base map, used everywhere

Use the same base map for every analysis layer: same scale, same orientation, same north arrow, same line weights. Each map then shows only its layer, such as movement in your accent colour, over the same faded base. The jury learns the map once and reads every layer instantly.

Figure-ground

Buildings in black, open space in white. It shows the grain of the area at a glance and sets up the argument for how your building fits it. See concept diagram ideas for using it before and after.

Movement and edges

Lines of different weights for busy and quiet routes, arrows for the main arrivals, a dashed line for service access. Mark the edges of the site by character: active street, quiet lane, garden, blank wall. This map usually explains your entrance and your plan's orientation.

Sun and wind

A sun-path diagram for the site's latitude, or simpler, the sun's angles in section at midsummer and midwinter, with the prevailing wind as arrows. Then say in one sentence what the data means for the design ("low winter sun from the south, so the courtyard opens south"). A wind rose is data; arrows showing the breeze reaching the courtyard are a finding.

A section through the site and the street

Site analysis is often drawn only in plan. One long section through the street, the site and its neighbours shows level changes, building heights, how far shade reaches and how the street meets the ground floor better than any map.

Photographs, with a key

A strip of three to six photographs, each numbered on a small key plan. Choose ones that show the findings: the busy edge, the old tree, the blank wall next door, the view you want to keep.

Activity over time

If the life of the place matters (markets, schools, stations, public spaces), map it at two or three times of day. It is often the most original part of an analysis because nobody else has it; you have to stand there and watch.

End with the move

The last diagram in the row shows the building's first form answering the findings: pulled back from the noise, opened to the view, shaded from the west.

How much space it gets

  • On a design jury board: usually a column or a band, roughly a fifth to a third of the sheet, often down the left side or across the top so it reads first. Our layout ideas show where a "left spine" of analysis fits.
  • On a thesis or urban project: often a whole board of its own, because the analysis is part of the argument.
  • In a jury deck: two or three slides after the idea and before the plans. The 10–15 slide deck guide places them.

What juries look for, and common mistakes

Jurors rarely ask for more analysis. They ask why. A good analysis lets you answer by pointing at a drawing. They look for:

  • A clear line from site to design. Each major move can be traced back to a finding.
  • Signs that you went there. Your own photographs, notes on activity at different times, things no map could tell you.
  • Accuracy. A north arrow, a scale, a sun path for the right latitude. A generic sun path or a wind rose copied from another city will be noticed, and makes jurors doubt the rest.
  • Selection. Five clear layers beat twelve postage stamps.
  • Honesty about trade-offs. If the view and the sun pull in different directions, say how you chose.

The usual mistakes are the opposite: maps at different scales and orientations that the jury has to relearn one by one, SWOT tables with nothing drawn behind them, and analysis that stops, where the plans never refer back to the findings. There is more in what architecture jurors look for.

Learn from buildings that answer their site

Look at how well-known buildings respond to their sites, and ask what the analysis must have found:

  • Salk Institute, La Jolla: the plaza between the laboratories opens to the Pacific, so the view of the ocean becomes the centre of the plan.
  • Magney House, Bingie Point, Australia: a long house one room deep, with louvres along the north side, which is the sunny side in the southern hemisphere.
  • Oslo Opera House: a marble roof that slopes into the fjord, so the building becomes a public place by the water.
  • Heritance Kandalama, Sri Lanka: a hotel laid along a rock face rather than placed on top of it, and left to be covered by the jungle.
  • Kanchanjunga Apartments, Mumbai: two-storey garden verandas that open the flats to the sea view and breeze from the west while shading them from the afternoon sun and monsoon rain.

Questions students ask

What should be included in an architecture site analysis?

Context and grain, access and movement, uses and activity, sun and climate, wind, views, ground and trees, noise, history and the planning rules. Study all of them, then present only the three to five findings that shaped your design, each on the same base map and paired with the design move it led to.

How do you present site analysis at a jury?

Briefly and as an argument. Show the site in context first, then the few findings that matter, each with its design response, before moving on to the plans. Two or three minutes, or two or three slides, is usually enough for a design jury.

Can I do a site analysis without visiting the site?

You can do part of it from maps, aerial images, street-level imagery and climate data, and for a site abroad that may be all you have. Say so on the board, and be careful with things you can only learn by being there, such as noise, activity at different times and how the place feels.

How many diagrams should a site analysis have?

For a design studio board, usually four to six: a context map, movement, sun and wind, views or edges, a site section, and a final diagram of the design's response. A thesis or urban project often needs more, sometimes a whole board.

Can I use Google Maps images in my site analysis?

It is safer to redraw your base map. Map screenshots are usually covered by the provider's terms. OpenStreetMap data is free to use with the credit "© OpenStreetMap contributors", and a redrawn base map also lets you control what it shows.
